# Reduction of Endomorphisms

Polynomial invariants turn a finite-dimensional endomorphism into a module over a polynomial ring. After the ideal theory of principal ideal domains and Cayley–Hamilton, this chapter develops primary decomposition, the commuting diagonalizable and nilpotent parts of a split endomorphism, Jordan chains, and conjugacy. Rational canonical form and the perfect-field Jordan–Chevalley theorem give complementary extensions of this main line.

## Principal Ideal Domain

!!! definition "Definition (Integral domain, PID)"
    <a id="def-re-1-1"></a>
    <a id="def-integral-domain-pid"></a>

    A commutative unitary ring $R$ is an **integral domain** if
    $1 \neq 0$ and $st \neq 0$ for all $s, t \neq 0$. An ideal of $R$ is
    **principal** if it is generated by a single element. A **principal
    ideal domain** (PID) is an integral domain in which every ideal is
    principal.

!!! definition "Definition (Prime / maximal ideal)"
    <a id="def-re-1-2"></a>
    <a id="def-prime-maximal-ideal"></a>

    An ideal $I \subsetneq R$ is **prime** if $R / I$ is an integral
    domain (equivalently $a, b \notin I \Rightarrow ab \notin I$). It is
    **maximal** if $R / I$ is a field. Every maximal ideal is prime. In a PID, a nonzero maximal ideal has an irreducible generator, by the converse prime-ideal criterion below.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Prime and maximal ideals in a PID)"
    <a id="prop-re-1-3"></a>

    <a id="prop-prime-and-maximal-ideals-in-a-pid"></a>

    1. If $a \in R$ is irreducible then the principal ideal $aR$ is
       maximal.

    2. If $a\ne0$ and $aR$ is prime, then $a$ is irreducible.

    3. Every prime ideal is contained in a maximal ideal (Zorn's
       lemma).

??? proof "Proof"
    1. Suppose $aR \subsetneq J \subsetneq R$ for an ideal $J$. Since
       $R$ is a PID, $J = bR$ for some $b \in R$. The inclusion
       $aR \subseteq bR$ gives $a = bc$ for some $c \in R$. Since
       $a$ is irreducible, $b$ is a unit (in which case $J = R$,
       contradiction) or $c$ is a unit, i.e. $bR = aR$ (so
       $J = aR$, contradiction). Hence no such $J$ exists; $aR$ is
       maximal.
    2. Suppose $a = bc$ with $b, c \in R \setminus R^\times$. Since
       $a \in aR$ and $aR$ is prime, $bc \in aR$ forces $b \in aR$ or
       $c \in aR$. Say $b = ad$, then $a = adc$, so $dc = 1$, i.e.
       $c$ is a unit, contradiction.
    3. Zorn's lemma applied to the set of ideals $J$ with
       $I \subseteq J \subsetneq R$, ordered by inclusion, gives a
       maximal element: the union of a chain is an ideal contained
       in $R$ and remains proper, because if its union contained $1$, one member would contain $1$. The union itself is therefore an upper bound in the set under consideration. The maximal
       element is a maximal ideal containing $I$.

!!! definition "Definition (Noetherian)"
    <a id="def-re-1-4"></a>
    <a id="def-noetherian"></a>

    A commutative ring $R$ is **Noetherian** if every strictly increasing
    chain of ideals $I_0 \subsetneq I_1 \subsetneq \cdots$ terminates. A
    PID is Noetherian (every ideal is generated by one element, so any
    chain is automatically stationary).

!!! theorem "Theorem (Unique factorisation in a PID)"
    <a id="thm-re-1-5"></a>
    <a id="thm-unique-factorisation-in-a-pid"></a>

    Let $A$ be a PID. Every non-zero $a \in A \setminus A^\times$ can be
    written as a product $a = p_1 \cdots p_n$ of irreducible elements,
    unique up to permutation and units. The factorisation in $K[T]$
    ($K$ a field) gives the **monic irreducible factorisation**
    $F(T) = c(F) \prod_{P} P(T)^{\mathrm{ord}_P(F)}$.

??? proof "Proof"
    **Existence.** If $a$ is irreducible, $a = a$ is the decomposition.
    Otherwise, the principal ideal $aA$ is properly contained in a
    maximal ideal ([Proposition](#noetherian-equivalences)), and this maximal ideal is $p_1A$ for a nonzero nonunit $p_1$. It is prime because its quotient is a field, so the prime-ideal criterion above makes $p_1$ irreducible. Thus
    $a = p_1 a_1$ with $a_1 \in A \setminus A^\times$. Iterate. The
    process must terminate: otherwise $aA \subsetneq a_1 A \subsetneq a_2 A \subsetneq \cdots$ is a strictly increasing chain of ideals,
    contradicting Noetherianness ([Proposition](#noetherian-equivalences)).
    **Uniqueness.** Induction on the minimal number of factors $n$.
    If $a$ is irreducible, any decomposition has exactly one factor
    (else $a = qr$ with $q, r$ non-units, contradicting irreducibility).
    For $n \geq 2$, suppose $a = p_1 \cdots p_n = q_1 \cdots q_m$. Since
    $p_n A$ is a prime ideal and $q_1 \cdots q_m \in p_n A$, some
    $q_j \in p_n A$. Permute so $q_m \in p_n A$, i.e. $q_m = \lambda p_n$
    for some $\lambda$. By [Definition](#pid-divisibility-associates), $q_m A = p_n A$. The remaining
    relation $p_1 \cdots p_{n-1} = \lambda' q_1 \cdots q_{m-1}$ has
    $n - 1$ factors on the left; by the induction hypothesis $n = m$
    and (up to a unit) the factors match.

!!! proposition "Proposition (GCD and LCM from prime factorisations)"
    <a id="prop-re-1-6"></a>
    <a id="prop-gcd-and-lcm-from-prime-factorisations"></a>

    For polynomials $F_1, \ldots, F_n$ in $K[T]$ and any monic
    irreducible $P$,

    $$
    \mathrm{ord}_P(\gcd(F_1, \ldots, F_n)) = \min_i \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i),
    \qquad
    \mathrm{ord}_P(\mathrm{lcm}(F_1, \ldots, F_n)) = \max_i
    \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i).
    $$

    Equivalently, $\gcd$ is the monic generator of
    $\sum_i F_i K[T]$ and $\mathrm{lcm}$ is the monic generator of
    $\bigcap_i F_i K[T]$.

??? proof "Proof"
    $\gcd(F_1, \ldots, F_n) \mid F_i$ gives
    $\mathrm{ord}_P(\gcd) \leq \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i)$ for all $i$, hence
    $\mathrm{ord}_P(\gcd) \leq \min_i \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i)$. Similarly
    $\mathrm{ord}_P(\mathrm{lcm}) \geq \max_i \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i)$.
    For the reverse inequality, set
    $G = \prod_P P^{\min_i \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i)}$. Then $G \mid F_i$ for
    all $i$, so $G$ divides every $K[T]$-linear combination, in
    particular $G \mid \gcd(F_1, \ldots, F_n)$, hence
    $\mathrm{ord}_P(\gcd) \geq \min_i \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i)$.
    Symmetrically, $L = \prod_P P^{\max_i \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i)}$ is a
    common multiple, so $\mathrm{lcm}(F_1, \ldots, F_n) \mid L$, giving
    the reverse inequality for the lcm.

!!! definition "Definition (Divisibility, associates and polynomial conventions)"
    <a id="pid-divisibility-associates"></a>

    In a commutative unital ring, $b\mid a$ means $a=bc$ for some $c$, equivalently $aR\subseteq bR$. In a domain, $aR=bR$ if and only if $a=ub$ for a unit $u$. For $P\ne0$, its degree and leading coefficient are denoted by $\deg P$ and $c(P)$, and it is monic if $c(P)=1$. The lecture convention is $\deg0=-\infty$ and the monic generator of the zero ideal is $0$. An irreducible element is a nonzero nonunit that is not a product of two nonunits.

??? proof "Proof"
    A unit factor gives both ideal inclusions. Conversely write $a=bx$ and $b=ay$. If $a=0$, then $b=0$ and the unit $1$ works. Otherwise $a=ayx$ and cancellation gives $yx=1$, so $x$ is a unit.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Polynomial PID and maximal-ideal criterion)"
    <a id="polynomial-pid-maximal-criterion"></a>

    If $K$ is a domain, $K[T]$ is a domain; if $K$ is a field, $K[T]$ is a PID and every ideal has a unique monic generator. In any commutative unital ring, an ideal is maximal precisely when it is maximal among the proper ideals.

??? proof "Proof"
    The leading term of a product of nonzero polynomials is nonzero, proving the domain assertion. In a nonzero ideal over a field choose a polynomial $P$ of minimal degree. Divide each $F$ in the ideal by $P$; the remainder is still in the ideal and has smaller degree, so is zero. Rescale $P$ to be monic. Two monic generators differ by a unit constant, which must be one. The zero ideal has generator zero.

    If $R/I$ is a field and $J$ properly contains $I$, take $a\in J\setminus I$ and lift an inverse $b$ of its class. Then $1-ab\in I$ and $1\in J$, so $J=R$. Conversely, maximality of a proper $I$ gives $I+aR=R$ for every $a\notin I$. Writing $1=c+ab$ with $c\in I$ supplies an inverse to the nonzero class of $a$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Noetherian equivalences and maximal ideals)"
    <a id="noetherian-equivalences"></a>

    The ascending-chain condition for ideals is equivalent to finite generation of every ideal. Principal ideal rings are therefore Noetherian. Every proper ideal of a Noetherian ring is contained in a maximal ideal; with the axiom of choice the same holds in every commutative unital ring.

??? proof "Proof"
    An ideal that is not finitely generated allows the recursive choice of an element outside the ideal generated by all previous choices, producing a strictly ascending chain. Conversely the union $I$ of an ascending sequence of ideals is an ideal; its finitely many generators all lie in one term, and that term and every subsequent term equal $I$. If a proper ideal were contained in no maximal ideal, one could repeatedly enlarge it while staying proper, violating this condition. In general apply Zorn to the proper ideals containing the given ideal: a chain's union is an ideal and remains proper since membership of $1$ would already occur in one chain member.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Ideal lattice, Bezout identity and valuations)"
    <a id="ideal-lattice-bezout-valuations"></a>

    Ideals form a complete lattice: infima are intersections (empty intersection $R$), and suprema are ideals generated by unions. In $K[T]$, the gcd of $F_1,\ldots,F_n$ is the monic generator of their ideal sum and satisfies a Bezout identity $\gcd(F_1,\ldots,F_n)=\sum_i A_iF_i$; the lcm generates their ideal intersection. For a monic irreducible $P$, set $\operatorname{ord}_P(0)=+\infty$. For nonzero polynomials,

    $$
    \operatorname{ord}_P(FG)=\operatorname{ord}_P(F)+\operatorname{ord}_P(G),
    \qquad
    \operatorname{ord}_P(F+G)\ge\min(\operatorname{ord}_P(F),\operatorname{ord}_P(G)).
    $$

    Moreover $F\mid G$ if and only if every exponent for $F$ is at most the corresponding exponent for $G$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Intersections and generated unions plainly give greatest lower and least upper bounds. Membership of the gcd in the finite ideal sum is exactly the Bezout identity. The product formula is uniqueness of factorization; the sum inequality follows by factoring out the common power of $P$. Divisibility follows by subtracting the nonnegative prime exponents, with the ratio of leading coefficients supplying the unit factor. The gcd/lcm formulas above extend to zero inputs by the stated infinite-exponent convention; all-zero gcd and any lcm with a zero input are zero.

## Cayley–Hamilton

!!! definition "Definition (Characteristic polynomial)"
    <a id="def-re-2-1"></a>
    <a id="def-characteristic-polynomial"></a>

    Let $K$ be a commutative ring, $V$ a free $K$-module of rank $n$,
    and $\varphi \in \mathrm{End}_K(V)$. The **characteristic polynomial**
    is
    $P_\varphi(T) = \det(T\, \mathrm{Id}_V - \varphi) \in K[T]$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Cayley–Hamilton)"
    <a id="thm-re-2-2"></a>
    <a id="thm-cayleyhamilton"></a>

    $P_\varphi(\varphi) = 0$ in $\mathrm{End}_K(V)$.

??? proof "Proof"
    View $V$ as a $K[T]$-module via $F \cdot x = F(\varphi)(x)$.
    Then $K[T] \otimes_K V$ is a free $K[T]$-module with basis
    $1 \otimes e_i$ ([Proposition](#extension-scalars-polynomial-action)). The endomorphism $T \otimes \mathrm{Id} - 1 \otimes \varphi$ of $K[T] \otimes V$ is the
    $K[T]$-linearisation of $T - \varphi$. By the adjugate identity
    (applied to the matrix $T \cdot \mathrm{Id} - \varphi$ over the
    commutative ring $K[T]$), it factors as
    $(T - \varphi) \circ (T - \varphi)^{\mathrm{adj}}$, with
    $(T - \varphi)^{\mathrm{adj}} \in M_n(K[T])$ and
    $(T - \varphi)(T - \varphi)^{\mathrm{adj}} = P_\varphi(T) \, \mathrm{Id}$ (the matrix identity $A \cdot \mathrm{adj}(A) = \det(A) \mathrm{Id}$). Now
    $P_\varphi(T) \otimes x = (T - \varphi)((T - \varphi)^{\mathrm{adj}} (1 \otimes x))$ for any $x \in V$. Map back to $V$ via
    $K[T] \otimes V \to V$, $F(T) \otimes y \mapsto F(\varphi)(y)$.
    The map is $K[T]$-linear, so the image of
    $(T - \varphi)(\xi)$ vanishes: this is exactly
    $T \cdot F(\varphi)(y) - \varphi(F(\varphi)(y)) = 0$ (i.e. the
    left side of $P_\varphi(\varphi)(y) = 0$). Hence
    $P_\varphi(\varphi)(y) = 0$ for all $y$, i.e.
    $P_\varphi(\varphi) = 0$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Adjugate identity: $A \cdot \mathrm{adj}(A) = \det(A) \mathrm{Id}$)"
    <a id="prop-re-2-3"></a>
    <a id="prop-adjugate-identity"></a>

    For any square matrix $A \in M_n(R)$ over a commutative ring $R$,

    $$
    A \cdot \mathrm{adj}(A) \;=\; \mathrm{adj}(A) \cdot A \;=\;
    \det(A)\, \mathrm{Id}_n,
    $$

    where $\mathrm{adj}(A)_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} \det(A^{(j,i)})$ is the
    classical adjugate (cofactor transpose).

??? proof "Proof"
    Laplace expansion shows that the $(i,j)$ entry of $A\operatorname{adj}(A)$ is the determinant of the matrix obtained by replacing row $j$ by row $i$. For $i=j$ it is $\det A$; otherwise the determinant vanishes because two rows coincide. Column expansion gives the reversed product. This applies over any commutative unital ring and needs no inverse.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Extension of scalars and polynomial action)"
    <a id="extension-scalars-polynomial-action"></a>

    If $A$ is a unital $K$-algebra, $A\otimes_K V$ is a left $A$-module by $a(b\otimes x)=ab\otimes x$. A basis $(e_i)$ of a finite free $V$ becomes the basis $(1\otimes e_i)$ over $A$. Polynomial evaluation $F\mapsto F(\varphi)$ is a unital algebra homomorphism, so polynomial expressions in $\varphi$ commute. It gives $V$ a $K[T]$-module structure and the $K[T]$-linear evaluation map $F\otimes x\mapsto F(\varphi)x$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Bilinearity of multiplication descends through the tensor relations and proves the module axioms. Given $\sum_i a_i\otimes e_i$, the coordinate maps $\mathrm{Id}_A\otimes e_i^\vee$ recover $a_i$, proving independence, while expansion of $x$ in the basis proves spanning. Equivalently the natural bijections $\operatorname{Hom}_A(A\otimes V,M)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_K(V,M)\cong M^n$ prove freeness. Distributivity and multiplication of powers show $(FG)(\varphi)=F(\varphi)G(\varphi)$; this also verifies the linearity of the evaluation map with respect to the stated polynomial action.

!!! lemma "Lemma (Duality and adjugates)"
    <a id="duality-adjugates"></a>

    For endomorphisms of a finite free module, $(\varphi^a)^\vee=(\varphi^\vee)^a$. If $\varphi^\vee=\psi^\vee$, then $\varphi=\psi$. Consequently $\varphi\varphi^a=(\det\varphi)\mathrm{Id}$ as well as $\varphi^a\varphi=(\det\varphi)\mathrm{Id}$.

??? proof "Proof"
    In dual bases the dual matrix is the transpose. Deleting a row and column commutes with transposition, and the determinant of a matrix equals that of its transpose. Thus the transposed cofactor matrices satisfy the first equality; equivalently evaluate the defining adjugate wedge against the wedge of the dual basis, retaining the same signs. The coordinate forms separate vectors: $x=\sum_i e_i^\vee(x)e_i$, so equality of all composed coordinate forms gives equality of the maps. Apply the adjugate identity to $\varphi^\vee$ and use this faithful duality, reversal of compositions, and $\det\varphi^\vee=\det\varphi$ to get the final identity.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Characteristic coefficients and trace)"
    <a id="characteristic-coefficients-trace"></a>

    For rank $n$ write

    $$
    P_\varphi(T)=T^n-s_1(\varphi)T^{n-1}+\cdots+(-1)^ns_n(\varphi).
    $$

    Here $s_1(\varphi)=\operatorname{tr}\varphi$ and $s_n(\varphi)=\det\varphi$. If the polynomial splits with roots $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ counted with multiplicity, $s_\ell$ is their $\ell$-th elementary symmetric function. Each $s_\ell$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $\ell$ in the matrix coordinates of $\varphi$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Expand $(Te_1-\varphi e_1)\wedge\cdots\wedge(Te_n-\varphi e_n)$. Choosing no transformed factors gives the leading coefficient one. Choosing all factors gives $(-1)^n\det\varphi$. Choosing one gives $-\sum_i a_{ii}$, the trace. More generally,

    $$
    s_\ell(\varphi)\eta=\sum_{|I|=\ell}z_1^I\wedge\cdots\wedge z_n^I,
    \qquad z_j^I=\begin{cases}\varphi(e_j)&j\in I,\\e_j&j\notin I.\end{cases}
    $$

    Every surviving wedge coefficient is a product of $\ell$ matrix entries, proving homogeneity. More explicitly, in a fixed basis replace the transformed factors, in increasing positions of $I$, successively by $\varphi_1(e_{i_1}),\ldots,\varphi_\ell(e_{i_\ell})$ and take the coefficient of $\eta$. This defines an $\ell$-linear form whose diagonal is $s_\ell$. Multiplication of the split polynomial gives the symmetric-function formula.

## Reduction of Linear Endomorphisms

!!! theorem "Theorem (Rational canonical form)"
    <a id="thm-re-3-1"></a>
    <a id="thm-rational-canonical-form"></a>

    Let $K$ be a field, $V$ a finite-dimensional $K$-vector space, and
    $\varphi \in \mathrm{End}_K(V)$. There is a decomposition
    $V = V_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus V_r$ into $\varphi$-invariant subspaces
    such that on each $V_i$, $\varphi$ is a cyclic endomorphism with
    minimal polynomial $P_i$ satisfying $P_1 \mid P_2 \mid \cdots \mid P_r$. Equivalently, there is a basis in which the matrix of $\varphi$
    is block-diagonal with companion matrices of the $P_i$.

??? proof "Proof"
    View $V$ as a $K[T]$-module via $T \cdot v = \varphi(v)$. Since
    $K[T]$ is a PID and $V$ is finitely generated (dim $V = n$),
    the structure theorem for modules over a PID gives a direct-sum
    decomposition $V \cong K[T]/(P_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus K[T]/(P_r)$
    with $P_1 \mid P_2 \mid \cdots \mid P_r$ non-zero polynomials (the
    **invariant factors**). Each summand $K[T]/(P_i)$ is a cyclic
    $K[T]$-module generated by some $v_i$ with annihilator $(P_i)$,
    i.e. $V_i = K[T] v_i$ is $\varphi$-cyclic of minimal polynomial
    $P_i$. The matrix of $\varphi$ in the basis $(v_i, T v_i, T^2 v_i, \ldots, T^{\deg P_i - 1} v_i)$ is the companion matrix of $P_i$.
    Block-diagonally, this gives the rational canonical form.
    **Smith normal form argument.** Alternatively, fix a basis of $V$
    and view $T \mathrm{Id} - \varphi \in M_n(K[T])$. Reduce by elementary
    row and column operations (over $K[T]$, which is a PID) to the
    Smith form, which exhibits the invariant factors on the diagonal.

??? proof "Proof"
    **The polynomial presentation and Smith reduction.** Fix a basis of $V$, with matrix $A$ for $\varphi$, and define $\pi:K[T]^n\to V$ by $\pi(\sum_j T^jv_j)=\sum_j\varphi^jv_j$. This is a surjective $K[T]$-linear map. Its kernel is $(T\mathrm{Id}-A)K[T]^n$: the inclusion follows by evaluation, and the identity

    $$
    T^jv-A^jv=(T\mathrm{Id}-A)\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}T^{j-1-k}A^kv
    $$

    reduces every polynomial vector to its constant remainder $\pi(v)$. A vector in the kernel therefore belongs to the stated image. The quotient and evaluation maps are inverse after this reduction.

    To reduce a matrix over $K[T]$, move a nonzero entry to the upper-left corner. Divide the other entries of its column by this pivot and subtract multiples of the first row. A nonzero remainder can be swapped into the pivot position and strictly lowers its degree. Do the same for its row by column operations. Once the pivot divides its entire row and column, clear them. If it fails to divide an entry in the remaining block, add that entry's column to the first column; another division produces a nonzero remainder of smaller degree. Thus finitely many degree decreases produce a pivot dividing every entry of the remaining block. Repeat inside that block. Every operation is invertible, and its entries remain multiples of the previous pivot. The resulting nonzero diagonal entries satisfy $d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots$. Multiplication by unit constants makes them monic.

    The monic invariant factors are unique. For each $k$, the ideal generated by all $k$-rowed minors is unchanged by an elementary row or column operation: multilinear expansion writes each new minor as a linear combination of old minors, and the inverse operation gives the reverse inclusion. For a diagonal matrix with the divisibility chain, this ideal is generated by $d_1\cdots d_k$, since that product is a minor and divides every other minor. Its monic generator therefore recovers each successive $d_k$ by division. This also proves invariance under the invertible presentation changes used here.

    For $T\mathrm{Id}-A$, the determinant is monic of degree $n$, so no diagonal entry is zero. Invertible row operations identify quotient targets, and invertible column operations preserve the image submodule. Hence the presentation gives $V\cong\bigoplus_iK[T]/(d_i)$. Unit entries give zero summands and may be discarded. In each remaining summand, polynomial division gives the basis $1,T,\ldots,T^{\deg d_i-1}$. Multiplication by $T$ has the companion matrix, its annihilator is $(d_i)$, and the displayed isomorphism sends these basis classes to the required vectors of $V$. This supplies the structure-theorem argument above with an explicit construction.

!!! definition "Definition (Elementary divisors, minimal / characteristic polynomial)"
    <a id="def-re-3-2"></a>
    <a id="def-elementary-divisors-minimal-characteristic-polynomial"></a>

    + The **minimal polynomial** $\mu_\varphi$ of $\varphi$ is the
      monic generator of the ideal $\{F \in K[T] : F(\varphi) = 0\}$.
    + The **elementary divisors** are the monic irreducible factors
      (with their powers) appearing in the invariant factors.
    + The **characteristic polynomial** $P_\varphi$ has the same
      elementary divisors as $\mu_\varphi$, with multiplicities equal to
      their multiplicities across all invariant factors: $P_\varphi=\prod_iP_i$, whereas $\mu_\varphi=P_r$ when $V\ne0$. Thus $\deg P_\varphi=\dim V$; for $V=0$ both polynomials are $1$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Cayley–Hamilton and minimal polynomial)"
    <a id="thm-re-3-3"></a>
    <a id="thm-cayleyhamilton-and-minimal-polynomial"></a>

    $\mu_\varphi \mid P_\varphi$ and $P_\varphi$ annihilates $\varphi$;
    $\mu_\varphi$ is the lcm of the invariant factors and the largest
    power of each irreducible appearing among them.

??? proof "Proof"
    By [Theorem](#thm-cayleyhamilton), $P_\varphi$ annihilates $\varphi$ (Cayley–Hamilton).
    By definition $\mu_\varphi$ is the monic generator of the
    annihilator ideal $\{\mu : \mu(\varphi) = 0\}$, so $\mu_\varphi \mid P_\varphi$ (since $P_\varphi(\varphi) = 0$).
    The characteristic polynomial is the product of the invariant
    factors (its roots are the eigenvalues counted with multiplicity),
    and the minimal polynomial is the lcm of the invariant factors
    (the largest $K[T]$-submodule of $K[T]^n$ that kills all of
    $V$); equivalently, the largest power of each irreducible
    appearing in the elementary divisors.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Restriction, invariant sums and characteristic factors)"
    <a id="invariant-subspace-polynomials"></a>

    If $W$ is $\varphi$-invariant, the minimal polynomial of its restriction divides $\mu_\varphi$. If invariant subspaces $V_i$ sum to $V$, $\mu_\varphi$ is the lcm of their minimal polynomials. If their sum is direct, $P_\varphi=\prod_iP_{\varphi|V_i}$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Every polynomial annihilating $\varphi$ annihilates its restriction. Conversely the lcm annihilates every $V_i$ and hence their sum; these two divisibilities prove equality of the monic polynomials. In a direct sum the union of the component bases is a basis of $V$. The characteristic wedge splits into the successive component wedges, multiplying their scalar factors.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Coprime kernel decomposition)"
    <a id="coprime-kernel-decomposition"></a>

    For pairwise coprime nonzero polynomials $F_i$ with product $F$, $\ker F(\varphi)=\bigoplus_i\ker F_i(\varphi)$. These kernels are invariant.

??? proof "Proof"
    Put $G_i=F/F_i$. The gcd of the $G_i$ is one, so choose $A_i$ with $\sum_iA_iG_i=1$. For $x\in\ker F(\varphi)$, set $x_i=A_i(\varphi)G_i(\varphi)x$. Then $\sum_i x_i=x$ and $F_i(\varphi)x_i=A_i(\varphi)F(\varphi)x=0$. If $x=\sum_i y_i$ with $F_i(\varphi)y_i=0$, applying $A_i(\varphi)G_i(\varphi)$ kills every $y_j$ with $j\ne i$. Applying the Bezout identity to $y_i$ shows the remaining value is $y_i$. Thus the expression is unique and the sum direct. Invariance follows because polynomial expressions commute with $\varphi$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Minimal and characteristic polynomial factors)"
    <a id="minimal-characteristic-factors"></a>

    For $d=\dim V$, $\mu_\varphi\mid P_\varphi\mid\mu_\varphi^d$. Thus these polynomials have the same irreducible factors. For $\lambda\in K$, being an eigenvalue is equivalent to being a root of either polynomial.

??? proof "Proof"
    Cayley–Hamilton proves the first divisibility. Write $\mu(T)=\sum_{k=0}^m a_kT^k$. Over $K[T]$, since $\mu(\varphi)=0$,

    $$
    \mu(T)\mathrm{Id}=(T\mathrm{Id}-\varphi)
    \sum_{k=1}^m a_k\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}T^{k-1-j}\varphi^j.
    $$

    Taking determinants proves the second divisibility. An eigenvector generates an invariant line with minimal polynomial $T-\lambda$, which divides $\mu_\varphi$. Conversely, if $\mu_\varphi=(T-\lambda)F$, minimality implies $F(\varphi)\ne0$. Choose $y$ with $x=F(\varphi)y\ne0$; then $(\varphi-\lambda)x=0$. The factor equivalence gives the characteristic-polynomial assertion.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Diagonalizability criterion)"
    <a id="diagonalizability-criterion"></a>

    An endomorphism is diagonalizable over $K$ if and only if its minimal polynomial is a product of distinct linear factors over $K$. In that case it equals $\prod_{\lambda\in\operatorname{Sp}(\varphi)}(T-\lambda)$.

??? proof "Proof"
    For distinct linear factors, the coprime kernel theorem decomposes $V$ into its eigenspaces, and their bases give an eigenbasis. Conversely decompose an eigenbasis into its invariant lines. The lcm formula gives the product of the distinct $T-\lambda$ appearing on those lines. Every eigenvalue has an invariant line and its factor must divide the minimal polynomial, proving the stated spectrum formula. For the zero space the empty product is one.

## Dunford Decomposition

!!! definition "Definition (Semisimple, nilpotent)"
    <a id="def-re-4-1"></a>
    <a id="def-semisimple-nilpotent"></a>

    An endomorphism $\varphi$ is **semisimple** (or **diagonalisable**)
    if $V$ decomposes as a direct sum of eigenspaces for $\varphi$. It
    is **nilpotent** if some power $\varphi^k = 0$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Dunford decomposition)"
    <a id="thm-re-4-2"></a>
    <a id="thm-dunford-decomposition"></a>

    Over any field $K$, suppose $P_\varphi(T)=\prod_{i=1}^r(T-\lambda_i)^{a_i}$ with distinct $\lambda_i\in K$. There is a unique decomposition $\varphi=S+N$ with $S$ diagonalizable over $K$, $N$ nilpotent, and $SN=NS$. Both parts are polynomials in $\varphi$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Put $F_i=(T-\lambda_i)^{a_i}$, $G_i=P_\varphi/F_i$, and choose $A_i$ with $\sum_iA_iG_i=1$. The kernel theorem gives $V=\bigoplus_i V_i$, $V_i=\ker F_i(\varphi)$, and the operators $\pi_i=A_i(\varphi)G_i(\varphi)$ are its projections: they are identity on $V_i$ and zero on every other $V_j$. Thus $\pi_i^2=\pi_i$, $\pi_i\pi_j=0$ for $i\ne j$, and $\sum_i\pi_i=\mathrm{Id}$.

    Set $S=\sum_i\lambda_i\pi_i$ and $N=\varphi-S$. On $V_i$, $S=\lambda_i\mathrm{Id}$ and $N=\varphi-\lambda_i\mathrm{Id}$, so $S$ has an eigenbasis and $N^d=0$ for $d=\dim V$. Both are polynomials in $\varphi$, hence commute.

    If $\varphi=S'+N'$ is another such decomposition, both $S'$ and $N'$ commute with $\varphi$. On an eigenvector $x$ of $S'$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, $(\varphi-\lambda)^d x=(N')^d x=0$. Thus $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $\varphi$ and belongs to the list $\lambda_i$. The generalized kernels $\ker(\varphi-\lambda_i)^d$ equal $V_i$: the primary decomposition already exhausts $V$, and on every other primary component $\varphi-\lambda_i$ is invertible by Bezout. Therefore $E_{\lambda_i}(S')\subseteq V_i$. Since the eigenvectors of $S'$ span $V$, equality holds on each component, so $S'=\sum_i\lambda_i\pi_i=S$ and $N'=N$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Nilpotency index bound)"
    <a id="nilpotency-index-bound"></a>

    If $\varphi$ is nilpotent on a $d$-dimensional space, $\varphi^d=0$ (for $d=0$, the identity is the zero endomorphism).

??? proof "Proof"
    Its minimal polynomial divides a power of $T$, and hence its characteristic polynomial has only the irreducible factor $T$. Monicity and degree force $P_\varphi=T^d$. Cayley–Hamilton proves the assertion.

## Jordan–Chevalley Decomposition

!!! theorem "Theorem (Jordan–Chevalley over a perfect field)"
    <a id="thm-re-5-1"></a>
    <a id="thm-jordanchevalley-over-a-perfect-field"></a>

    Over a perfect field there is a unique commuting sum of a geometrically semisimple part and a nilpotent part, both polynomial in $\varphi$. Here geometrically semisimple means diagonalizable after extending to an algebraic closure; it need not be diagonalizable over $K$. The following Jordan-block theorem uses the split hypothesis and the lecture's definition of semisimplicity over $K$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Factor the minimal polynomial as $\prod_i q_i^{e_i}$. Perfectness makes each irreducible $q_i$ separable, so $q_i'$ is invertible modulo $q_i$. In $B_i=K[T]/(q_i^{e_i})$, start with $s=T$ and repeatedly replace $s$ by $s-q_i(s)q_i'(s)^{-1}$. The derivative remains invertible since $s\equiv T\pmod {q_i}$. Polynomial expansion shows that each iteration squares the ideal in which the error $q_i(s)$ lies; after finitely many iterations it is zero. Meanwhile $s-T\in(q_i)$, so this difference is nilpotent. Chinese remainders combine the $s$ into a polynomial class modulo the minimal polynomial. Evaluate it at $\varphi$ to obtain $S$ and let $N=\varphi-S$. They commute, $N$ is nilpotent on each primary component, and $\prod_iq_i(S)=0$. This product is square-free and splits over an algebraic closure, so $S$ becomes diagonalizable there by the diagonalizability criterion. Uniqueness follows after that scalar extension from the split Dunford theorem, and equality after scalar extension implies equality of the original matrices.

!!! definition "Definition (Jordan endomorphism and Jordan block)"
    <a id="jordan-endomorphism-block"></a>

    A Jordan endomorphism on a nonzero $d$-dimensional space has characteristic and minimal polynomials both equal to $(T-\lambda)^d$. It has a basis $x,\psi x,\ldots,\psi^{d-1}x$, where $\psi=\varphi-\lambda\mathrm{Id}$ and $\psi^{d-1}x\ne0$. In the row-vector convention its matrix has $\lambda$ on the diagonal and ones just above it.

!!! lemma "Lemma (Independence of a nilpotent chain)"
    <a id="nilpotent-chain-independence"></a>

    If $\psi^r x=0$ and $\psi^{r-1}x\ne0$, then $x,\psi x,\ldots,\psi^{r-1}x$ are independent.

??? proof "Proof"
    Apply $\psi^{r-1}$ to a relation to kill all but the coefficient of $x$. That coefficient is zero. Apply $\psi^{r-2}$ to the remaining relation to kill all but the coefficient of $\psi x$, and continue. Every coefficient vanishes. A chain of length $d$ consequently forms a basis; the equations $\varphi\psi^jx=\lambda\psi^jx+\psi^{j+1}x$ give the stated matrix.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Decomposition into Jordan blocks)"
    <a id="jordan-block-decomposition"></a>

    If the minimal polynomial splits into linear factors over $K$, $V$ is a direct sum of invariant subspaces on each of which the restriction is a Jordan endomorphism.

??? proof "Proof"
    The coprime kernel decomposition reduces to $\mu_\varphi=(T-\lambda)^r$. Put $\psi=\varphi-\lambda\mathrm{Id}$ and choose $x$ with $\psi^{r-1}x\ne0$. Let $W$ be its length-$r$ chain span. Choose a linear form $f$ with $f(\psi^{r-1}x)\ne0$, and let $H$ be spanned in $V^\vee$ by $f,f\psi,\ldots,f\psi^{r-1}$. The same chain lemma for $\psi^\vee$ makes these $r$ forms independent, so their common annihilator $U$ has dimension $\dim V-r$.

    If $w=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}b_j\psi^jx$ is nonzero, let $j_0$ be its smallest index with $b_{j_0}\ne0$. Then $f\psi^{r-1-j_0}(w)=b_{j_0}f(\psi^{r-1}x)\ne0$, so $w\notin U$. Hence $U\cap W=0$ and the dimensions give $V=U\oplus W$. The space $H$ is stable under composition with $\psi$, so $U$ is $\psi$-invariant and therefore $\varphi$-invariant. Induct on dimension on $U$; on $W$ the full chain gives a Jordan block. Apply the construction to each primary component.

!!! corollary "Corollary (Counting Jordan blocks)"
    <a id="counting-jordan-blocks"></a>

    For the nilpotent part $N$ of the Dunford decomposition, $\dim\ker N$ is the total number of blocks, and $\dim\ker N^i-\dim\ker N^{i-1}$ is the number of blocks of size at least $i$.

??? proof "Proof"
    A block of size $r$ contributes $\min(i,r)$ to $\dim\ker N^i$. Sum over the direct sum and subtract consecutive values.

## Behaviour under Conjugation

!!! proposition "Proposition (Invariants of similarity)"
    <a id="prop-re-6-1"></a>
    <a id="prop-invariants-of-similarity"></a>

    If $\psi = g \varphi g^{-1}$ for some $g \in \mathrm{GL}(V)$, then

    1. $\det(\psi) = \det(\varphi)$ and $\mathrm{tr}(\psi) = \mathrm{tr}(\varphi)$.
    2. $P_\psi = P_\varphi$ and $\mu_\psi = \mu_\varphi$.
    3. The elementary divisors of $\psi$ equal those of $\varphi$.

    In particular, the rational canonical form, the Dunford
    decomposition, and the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition are
    invariants of the similarity class of $\varphi$.

??? proof "Proof"
    1. $\det(\psi) = \det(g \varphi g^{-1}) = \det g \cdot \det \varphi \cdot \det g^{-1} = \det \varphi$. For the trace, choose a basis
       of $V$ and compute; the conjugation $g$ acts on matrices as
       $\psi = P\varphi P^{-1}$, which has the same trace as $\varphi$
       (cyclic property of trace).
    2. $P_\psi(T) = \det(T \mathrm{Id} - \psi) = \det(T \mathrm{Id} - g \varphi g^{-1}) = \det(g (T \mathrm{Id} - \varphi) g^{-1}) = \det(T \mathrm{Id} - \varphi) = P_\varphi(T)$. The minimal
       polynomial satisfies $\mu_\psi(\psi)=g\mu_\psi(\varphi)g^{-1}$ (since the polynomial
       coefficients commute with $g$). So $\mu_\psi(\varphi) = 0$ iff
       $\mu_\psi(\psi) = 0$, i.e. $\mu_\psi = \mu_\varphi$.
    3. The Smith normal form of the matrix $T \mathrm{Id} - \varphi$ (in $K[T]^{n \times n}$) gives the invariant factors
       ([Theorem](#thm-rational-canonical-form)), which are determined by the elementary divisors.
       Conjugation by $g$ corresponds to changing the basis of $V$
       on both sides of the matrix, i.e. replacing $T \mathrm{Id} - \varphi$ by $T \mathrm{Id} - \psi = g (T \mathrm{Id} - \varphi) g^{-1}$, which is the same $K[T]$-module up to isomorphism.
       The Smith normal form is invariant under this.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Simultaneous diagonalization)"
    <a id="thm-re-6-2"></a>
    <a id="thm-simultaneous-diagonalization"></a>

    A commuting family of endomorphisms, each diagonalizable over $K$, has a common eigenbasis in finite dimension.

??? proof "Proof"
    Induct on the dimension. If every member is scalar, any basis works. Otherwise choose a nonscalar member and decompose $V$ into its proper eigenspaces. Commutativity makes each of these invariant under the whole family. Restrictions remain diagonalizable because their minimal polynomials divide the square-free split minimal polynomials of the original maps. Apply induction in each eigenspace and unite the resulting bases. A general commuting family does not in general possess a simultaneous rational canonical form; for example one Jordan block and its square need not share a basis in which both have their respective companion-block forms.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Characteristic polynomials of two compositions)"
    <a id="two-composition-characteristic-polynomials"></a>

    For $\varphi:V\to W$ and $\psi:W\to V$,

    $$
    P_{\psi\varphi}(T)T^{\dim W}=P_{\varphi\psi}(T)T^{\dim V}.
    $$

    In particular $\operatorname{tr}(\psi\varphi)=\operatorname{tr}(\varphi\psi)$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Define $\Phi(x,y)=(\psi\varphi x,\varphi x)$ on $V\oplus W$. Its block-triangular characteristic determinant is $P_{\psi\varphi}(T)T^{\dim W}$. The invertible map $g(x,y)=(x-\psi y,y)$ has inverse $(x+\psi y,y)$, and direct substitution gives $g\Phi g^{-1}(x,y)=(0,\varphi x+\varphi\psi y)$. Its characteristic determinant is $T^{\dim V}P_{\varphi\psi}(T)$. Conjugation preserves that determinant, proving equality. Compare the coefficients one degree below the leading coefficient to get the trace formula.
