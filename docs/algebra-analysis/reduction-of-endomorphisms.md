# Reduction of Endomorphisms

This chapter is the **linear-algebra capstone**: given an endomorphism
$\varphi$ of a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $K$, we
decompose it into a direct sum of cyclic blocks determined by the
**invariant factors** of $\varphi$, and read off the **Cayley–Hamilton
theorem**, the **Dunford decomposition** (commuting semisimple + nilpotent
parts), and the **Jordan–Chevalley decomposition** (polynomially commuting
semisimple + nilpotent). Everything rests on the structure theory of
**principal ideal domains**.

## Principal Ideal Domain

!!! definition "Definition (Integral domain, PID)"
    <a id="def-re-1-1"></a>
    A commutative unitary ring $R$ is an **integral domain** if
    $1 \neq 0$ and $st \neq 0$ for all $s, t \neq 0$. An ideal of $R$ is
    **principal** if it is generated by a single element. A **principal
    ideal domain** (PID) is an integral domain in which every ideal is
    principal.

!!! definition "Definition (Prime / maximal ideal)"
    <a id="def-re-1-2"></a>
    An ideal $I \subsetneq R$ is **prime** if $R / I$ is an integral
    domain (equivalently $a, b \notin I \Rightarrow ab \notin I$). It is
    **maximal** if $R / I$ is a field. Every maximal ideal is prime.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Prime and maximal ideals in a PID)"
    <a id="prop-re-1-3"></a>
    1. If $a \in R$ is irreducible then the principal ideal $aR$ is
       maximal.
    2. If $R$ is a PID and $aR$ is prime, then $a$ is irreducible.
    3. Every prime ideal is contained in a maximal ideal (Zorn's
       lemma).

!!! definition "Definition (Noetherian)"
    <a id="def-re-1-4"></a>
    A commutative ring $R$ is **Noetherian** if every strictly increasing
    chain of ideals $I_0 \subsetneq I_1 \subsetneq \cdots$ terminates. A
    PID is Noetherian (every ideal is generated by one element, so any
    chain is automatically stationary).

!!! theorem "Theorem (Unique factorisation in a PID)"
    <a id="thm-re-1-5"></a>
    Let $A$ be a PID. Every non-zero $a \in A \setminus A^\times$ can be
    written as a product $a = p_1 \cdots p_n$ of irreducible elements,
    unique up to permutation and units. The factorisation in $K[T]$
    ($K$ a field) gives the **monic irreducible factorisation**
    $F(T) = c(F) \prod_{P} P(T)^{\mathrm{ord}_P(F)}$.

??? proof "Proof (Unique factorisation in a PID)"
    **Existence.** If $a$ is irreducible, $a = a$ is the decomposition.
    Otherwise, the principal ideal $aA$ is properly contained in a
    maximal ideal (Prop 6.1.14), and by Prop 6.1.11 every maximal
    ideal in a PID is generated by an irreducible $p_1$. So
    $a = p_1 a_1$ with $a_1 \in A \setminus A^\times$. Iterate. The
    process must terminate: otherwise $aA \subsetneq a_1 A \subsetneq
    a_2 A \subsetneq \cdots$ is a strictly increasing chain of ideals,
    contradicting Noetherianness (Prop 6.1.13).
    **Uniqueness.** Induction on the minimal number of factors $n$.
    If $a$ is irreducible, any decomposition has exactly one factor
    (else $a = qr$ with $q, r$ non-units, contradicting irreducibility).
    For $n \geq 2$, suppose $a = p_1 \cdots p_n = q_1 \cdots q_m$. Since
    $p_n A$ is a prime ideal and $q_1 \cdots q_m \in p_n A$, some
    $q_j \in p_n A$. Permute so $q_m \in p_n A$, i.e. $q_m = \lambda p_n$
    for some $\lambda$. By Prop 6.1.3, $q_m A = p_n A$. The remaining
    relation $p_1 \cdots p_{n-1} = \lambda' q_1 \cdots q_{m-1}$ has
    $n - 1$ factors on the left; by the induction hypothesis $n = m$
    and (up to a unit) the factors match.
    <a id="prop-re-1-6"></a>
    For polynomials $F_1, \ldots, F_n$ in $K[T]$ and any monic
    irreducible $P$,

    $$
    \mathrm{ord}_P(\gcd(F_1, \ldots, F_n)) = \min_i \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i),
    \qquad
    \mathrm{ord}_P(\mathrm{lcm}(F_1, \ldots, F_n)) = \max_i
    \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i).
    $$

    Equivalently, $\gcd$ is the monic generator of
    $\sum_i F_i K[T]$ and $\mathrm{lcm}$ is the monic generator of
    $\bigcap_i F_i K[T]$.

??? proof "Proof (GCD, LCM from factorisations)"
    $\gcd(F_1, \ldots, F_n) \mid F_i$ gives
    $\mathrm{ord}_P(\gcd) \leq \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i)$ for all $i$, hence
    $\mathrm{ord}_P(\gcd) \leq \min_i \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i)$. Similarly
    $\mathrm{ord}_P(\mathrm{lcm}) \geq \max_i \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i)$.
    For the reverse inequality, set
    $G = \prod_P P^{\min_i \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i)}$. Then $G \mid F_i$ for
    all $i$, so $G$ divides every $K[T]$-linear combination, in
    particular $G \mid \gcd(F_1, \ldots, F_n)$, hence
    $\mathrm{ord}_P(\gcd) \geq \min_i \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i)$.
    Symmetrically, $L = \prod_P P^{\max_i \mathrm{ord}_P(F_i)}$ is a
    common multiple, so $\mathrm{lcm}(F_1, \ldots, F_n) \mid L$, giving
    the reverse inequality for the lcm.

## Cayley–Hamilton

!!! definition "Definition (Characteristic polynomial)"
    <a id="def-re-2-1"></a>
    Let $K$ be a commutative ring, $V$ a free $K$-module of rank $n$,
    and $\varphi \in \mathrm{End}_K(V)$. The **characteristic polynomial**
    is
    $P_\varphi(T) = \det(T\, \mathrm{Id}_V - \varphi) \in K[T]$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Cayley–Hamilton)"
    <a id="thm-re-2-2"></a>
    $P_\varphi(\varphi) = 0$ in $\mathrm{End}_K(V)$.

    **Proof.** View $V$ as a $K[T]$-module via $F \cdot x = F(\varphi)(x)$.
    Then $K[T] \otimes_K V$ is a free $K[T]$-module with basis
    $1 \otimes e_i$. The endomorphism $T \otimes \mathrm{Id} - 1 \otimes
    \varphi$ of $K[T] \otimes V$ factors as
    $(T - \varphi) \circ (T - \varphi)^{\mathrm{adj}}$ where
    $(T - \varphi)^{\mathrm{adj}}$ is the adjugate. Applied to
    $1 \otimes x \in K[T] \otimes V$, this yields
    $P_\varphi(T) \otimes x = (T - \varphi)(\xi)$ for some $\xi$;
    mapping back to $V$ kills $(T - \varphi)(\xi)$ and gives
    $P_\varphi(\varphi)(x) = 0$.

??? proof "Proof (Adjugate identity)"
    For $A \in M_n(K)$, $A \cdot \mathrm{adj}(A) = \det(A) \mathrm{Id}$. Apply
    it to $A = T \cdot \mathrm{Id} - \varphi$ in $M_n(K[T])$ to get
    $(T - \varphi) (T - \varphi)^{\mathrm{adj}} = P_\varphi(T)\, \mathrm{Id}$.

## Reduction of Linear Endomorphisms

!!! theorem "Theorem (Rational canonical form)"
    <a id="thm-re-3-1"></a>
    Let $K$ be a field, $V$ a finite-dimensional $K$-vector space, and
    $\varphi \in \mathrm{End}_K(V)$. There is a decomposition
    $V = V_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus V_r$ into $\varphi$-invariant subspaces
    such that on each $V_i$, $\varphi$ is a cyclic endomorphism with
    minimal polynomial $P_i$ satisfying $P_1 \mid P_2 \mid \cdots \mid
    P_r$. Equivalently, there is a basis in which the matrix of $\varphi$
    is block-diagonal with companion matrices of the $P_i$.

    **Proof (sketch).** The $K[T]$-module $V$ has invariant factors
    obtained from a Smith normal form of the map $T \cdot \mathrm{Id} -
    \varphi : K[T]^n \to K[T]^n$. The structure theorem for finitely
    generated modules over a PID yields the direct sum of cyclic modules.

!!! definition "Definition (Elementary divisors, minimal / characteristic polynomial)"
    <a id="def-re-3-2"></a>
    + The **minimal polynomial** $\mu_\varphi$ of $\varphi$ is the
      monic generator of the ideal $\{F \in K[T] : F(\varphi) = 0\}$.
    + The **elementary divisors** are the monic irreducible factors
      appearing in the invariant factors.
    + The **characteristic polynomial** $P_\varphi$ has the same
      elementary divisors as $\mu_\varphi$, with multiplicities equal to
      $\dim V$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Cayley–Hamilton and minimal polynomial)"
    <a id="thm-re-3-3"></a>
    $\mu_\varphi \mid P_\varphi$ and $P_\varphi$ annihilates $\varphi$;
    $\mu_\varphi$ is the lcm of the invariant factors and the largest
    power of each irreducible appearing among them.

## Dunford Decomposition

!!! definition "Definition (Semisimple, nilpotent)"
    <a id="def-re-4-1"></a>
    An endomorphism $\varphi$ is **semisimple** (or **diagonalisable**)
    if $V$ decomposes as a direct sum of eigenspaces for $\varphi$. It
    is **nilpotent** if some power $\varphi^k = 0$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Dunford decomposition)"
    <a id="thm-re-4-2"></a>
    Let $K$ be a field of characteristic zero and $\varphi \in
    \mathrm{End}(V)$ a finite-dimensional endomorphism. There is a
    unique pair $(\varphi_s, \varphi_n)$ with $\varphi_s$ semisimple,
    $\varphi_n$ nilpotent, $\varphi_s \varphi_n = \varphi_n \varphi_s$,
    and $\varphi = \varphi_s + \varphi_n$.

??? proof "Proof (Dunford decomposition)"
    Factor the minimal polynomial $\mu_\varphi = \prod_{i=1}^{r}
    P_i^{e_i}$ as a product of powers of distinct monic irreducibles
    $P_1, \ldots, P_r$. For each $i$, set
    $V_i := \ker P_i(\varphi)^{e_i}$. By the primary decomposition
    theorem, $V = \bigoplus_i V_i$ and each $V_i$ is $\varphi$-invariant.
    Let $e_i \in \mathrm{End}(V)$ be the projector onto $V_i$ along
    $\bigoplus_{j \neq i} V_j$ (it is a polynomial in $\varphi$ by the
    Chinese remainder decomposition
    $K[T] / (\mu_\varphi) \cong \prod_i K[T] / (P_i^{e_i})$).
    On $V_i$, $P_i(\varphi)$ is nilpotent on $\ker P_i(\varphi)$ and
    vanishes on the quotient $V_i / \ker P_i(\varphi)$, so
    $\varphi|_{V_i}$ has a unique decomposition
    $\varphi|_{V_i} = \varphi_{s, i} + \varphi_{n, i}$ with
    $\varphi_{s, i}$ semisimple and $\varphi_{n, i}$ nilpotent, commuting.
    Set $\varphi_s = \sum_i \varphi_{s, i} \circ e_i$ and
    $\varphi_n = \sum_i \varphi_{n, i} \circ e_i$. Then $\varphi_s$ is
    semisimple, $\varphi_n$ is nilpotent, they commute, and
    $\varphi = \varphi_s + \varphi_n$. Uniqueness: any such
    decomposition restricts to a Dunford decomposition of each
    $\varphi|_{V_i}$, which is unique on each block.

## Jordan–Chevalley Decomposition

!!! theorem "Theorem (Jordan–Chevalley)"
    <a id="thm-re-5-1"></a>
    Let $K$ be a perfect field and $\varphi \in \mathrm{End}(V)$ a
    finite-dimensional endomorphism. There exists a unique pair
    $(\varphi_s, \varphi_n)$ of polynomial expressions in $\varphi$
    (i.e. $\varphi_s, \varphi_n \in K[\varphi]$) such that

    + $\varphi_s$ is semisimple, $\varphi_n$ is nilpotent;
    + $\varphi_s \varphi_n = \varphi_n \varphi_s$;
    + $\varphi = \varphi_s + \varphi_n$.

??? proof "Proof (Jordan–Chevalley decomposition)"
    Use the Dunford decomposition $\varphi = \varphi_s^{\mathrm{Dunf}} +
    \varphi_n^{\mathrm{Dunf}}$ (Thm 6-4-2) and construct a polynomial
    $Q \in K[T]$ with $Q(\lambda) = \lambda$ for every eigenvalue
    $\lambda$ of $\varphi$ and $Q^{(j)}(0) = 0$ for $j = 1, \ldots, d-1$
    where $d$ is the maximal nilpotency index of $\varphi_n$.
    Such a polynomial exists by the Chinese remainder theorem: the
    conditions amount to specifying $Q$ and its derivatives at finitely
    many points of $\overline{K}$. (The perfect-field hypothesis
    ensures that eigenvalues land in $K$ or, more generally, that
    the minimal polynomial splits into distinct linear factors after
    passing to a separable closure, so we can solve the interpolation
    problem in $K$.) Set $\varphi_s = Q(\varphi)$ and
    $\varphi_n = \varphi - \varphi_s$. The computation
    $\varphi_s = Q(\varphi)$ shows $\varphi_s \in K[\varphi]$; on each
    primary block, $Q(\varphi) = \lambda_i$ on the semisimple part and
    $Q(\varphi) = \lambda_i$ on the nilpotent part (by the
    Taylor-expansion argument), giving the Jordan–Chevalley form.
    Uniqueness: any polynomial $R$ satisfying these properties must
    agree with $Q$ on the spectrum of $\varphi$ by a Vandermonde
    argument on the eigenvalues.

## Behaviour under Conjugation

!!! proposition "Proposition (Invariants of similarity)"
    <a id="prop-re-6-1"></a>
    If $\psi = g \varphi g^{-1}$ for some $g \in \mathrm{GL}(V)$, then

    1. $\det(\psi) = \det(\varphi)$ and $\mathrm{tr}(\psi) = \mathrm{tr}(\varphi)$.
    2. $P_\psi = P_\varphi$ and $\mu_\psi = \mu_\varphi$.
    3. The elementary divisors of $\psi$ equal those of $\varphi$.

    In particular, the rational canonical form, the Dunford
    decomposition, and the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition are
    invariants of the similarity class of $\varphi$.

??? proof "Proof (Invariants of similarity)"
    1. $\det(\psi) = \det(g \varphi g^{-1}) = \det g \cdot \det \varphi
       \cdot \det g^{-1} = \det \varphi$. For the trace, choose a basis
       of $V$ and compute; the conjugation $g$ acts on matrices as
       $\psi = P_\varphi P^{-1}$, which has the same trace as $\varphi$
       (cyclic property of trace).
    2. $P_\psi(T) = \det(T \mathrm{Id} - \psi) = \det(T \mathrm{Id} -
       g \varphi g^{-1}) = \det(g (T \mathrm{Id} - \varphi) g^{-1}) =
       \det(T \mathrm{Id} - \varphi) = P_\varphi(T)$. The minimal
       polynomial satisfies $g \mu_\psi(\psi) g^{-1} = \mu_\psi(g
       \varphi g^{-1}) = \mu_\psi(\varphi)$ (since the polynomial
       coefficients commute with $g$). So $\mu_\psi(\varphi) = 0$ iff
       $\mu_\psi(\psi) = 0$, i.e. $\mu_\psi = \mu_\varphi$.
    3. The Smith normal form of the matrix $T \mathrm{Id} -
       \varphi$ (in $K[T]^{n \times n}$) gives the invariant factors
       (Thm 6.3.1), which are determined by the elementary divisors.
       Conjugation by $g$ corresponds to changing the basis of $V$
       on both sides of the matrix, i.e. replacing $T \mathrm{Id} -
       \varphi$ by $T \mathrm{Id} - \psi = g (T \mathrm{Id} - \varphi)
       g^{-1}$, which is the same $K[T]$-module up to isomorphism.
       The Smith normal form is invariant under this.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Simultaneous reduction of commuting operators)"
    <a id="thm-re-6-2"></a>
    A family of commuting diagonalisable endomorphisms of a
    finite-dimensional vector space over an algebraically closed field
    can be simultaneously diagonalised. More generally, a family of
    commuting operators has a simultaneous rational canonical form.

??? proof "Proof (Simultaneous reduction, sketch)"
    For a family of commuting diagonalisable operators over an
    algebraically closed field, use induction on $\dim V$. Pick
    $\varphi_1$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ and eigenspace
    $V_\lambda \neq 0$. The other operators $\varphi_i$ commute with
    $\varphi_1$, so they preserve $V_\lambda$. By induction each
    $\varphi_i|_{V_\lambda}$ is diagonalisable, and the same holds
    on the complementary quotient $V / V_\lambda$ (the operators
    descend to the quotient, which is again diagonalisable).
    Lifting an eigenbasis of $V / V_\lambda$ gives the
    simultaneous eigenbasis.
    For the general case (rational canonical form), use induction on
    $\dim V$ and the fact that any operator $T$ has a cyclic
    sub-decomposition. The joint invariant factors are obtained from
    the Smith normal form of a generating matrix, which is
    simultaneously computable for all commuting operators.
