# Self-adjoint Operators

This chapter develops the **geometry of inner-product spaces** and the
**operator theory of bounded self-adjoint linear maps** on a Hilbert space.
The cornerstone is the **orthogonal projection** onto a complete subspace,
which exists in any Hilbert space by the parallelogram identity. We then
study **adjoints** of bounded operators and the **spectral reduction** of
self-adjoint maps.

## Inner Product Spaces

!!! definition "Definition (Semi-inner / inner product)"
    <a id="def-sa-1-1"></a>
    A map $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : V \times V \to K$ on a vector
    space $V$ over $K \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}$ is a **semi-inner
    product** if

    1. $\langle x, \cdot \rangle$ is linear for every $x$;
    2. it is **conjugate symmetric**:
       $\langle y, x \rangle = \overline{\langle x, y \rangle}$;
    3. it is **semi-positive**: $\langle x, x \rangle \geq 0$.

    It is an **inner product** if additionally $\langle x, x \rangle > 0$
    for all $x \neq 0$. $(V, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ is then an
    **inner product space**.

!!! definition "Definition (Orthogonality, orthogonal complement)"
    <a id="def-sa-1-2"></a>
    In a semi-inner product space, $x \perp y$ iff $\langle x, y \rangle =
    0$. The **orthogonal complement** of $A \subseteq V$ is
    $A^\perp = \{y : \forall x \in A,\ \langle x, y \rangle = 0\}$, a
    vector subspace containing the subspace generated by $A$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Properties of the orthogonal complement)"
    <a id="prop-sa-1-3"></a>
    1. $A^\perp$ is a subspace; if $A \subseteq B$ then $B^\perp
       \subseteq A^\perp$.
    2. The subspace generated by $A$ is contained in $(A^\perp)^\perp$.
    3. If $W$ is the subspace generated by $A$, then $A^\perp = W^\perp$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Cauchy–Schwarz)"
    <a id="thm-sa-1-4"></a>
    For any $(x, y) \in V \times V$ in a semi-inner product space,
    $|\langle x, y \rangle|^2 \leq \langle x, x \rangle \langle y, y \rangle$.

??? proof "Proof (Cauchy–Schwarz)"
    If $\langle y, y \rangle = 0$, then for any $t \in K$ with $|t| = 1$,
    $0 \leq \langle x + t y, x + t y \rangle = \langle x, x \rangle +
    2 \mathrm{Re}(\bar t \langle x, y \rangle) + \langle y, y \rangle$.
    Choosing $t$ so that $\bar t \langle x, y \rangle = |\langle x, y \rangle|$
    gives $0 \leq \langle x, x \rangle + 2 |\langle x, y \rangle|$, so
    $|\langle x, y \rangle| \leq \langle x, x \rangle / 2$. Combined with
    $\langle y, y \rangle = 0$ this gives
    $|\langle x, y \rangle|^2 \leq 0 \cdot \langle x, x \rangle$.

    Otherwise $\langle y, y \rangle > 0$. Pick $u \in K$ with $|u| = 1$
    and $u \langle x, y \rangle = |\langle x, y \rangle|$. For any
    $t \in \mathbb{R}$,
    $0 \leq \langle x + t u y, x + t u y \rangle
    = \langle x, x \rangle + 2t \mathrm{Re}(\bar u \langle x, y \rangle)
    + t^2 \langle y, y \rangle
    = \langle x, x \rangle + 2t |\langle x, y \rangle| + t^2 \langle y, y \rangle$.
    This quadratic in $t$ is $\geq 0$ for all $t$, so its discriminant
    $4 |\langle x, y \rangle|^2 - 4 \langle x, x \rangle \langle y, y
    \rangle \leq 0$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Norm from an inner product)"
    <a id="prop-sa-1-5"></a>
    The map $\|x\| := \sqrt{\langle x, x \rangle}$ is a seminorm on $V$,
    and a norm iff $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ is an inner product.
    The kernel of $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ equals $\{x : \|x\| = 0\}$.
    For any subspace $W$ of the kernel, the quotient
    $(V / W, \langle [\cdot], [\cdot] \rangle^\sim)$ is a semi-inner
    product space with $\langle [x], [y] \rangle^\sim = \langle x, y
    \rangle$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Bounded evaluation)"
    <a id="prop-sa-1-6"></a>
    For an inner product space $V$, the linear form
    $\ell_x : V \to K$, $z \mapsto \langle x, z \rangle$ is bounded with
    $\|\ell_x\| = \|x\|$. In particular, $\ell_x = \ell_y$ iff $x = y$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Norm from a polarisation identity)"
    <a id="prop-sa-1-7"></a>
    A norm $\|\cdot\|$ on a real (resp. complex) vector space is induced
    by a (semi-)inner product iff the parallelogram identity

    $$
    \|x + y\|^2 + \|x - y\|^2 = 2 \|x\|^2 + 2 \|y\|^2
    $$

    holds for all $x, y$. In the real case the polarisation is

    $$
    \langle x, y \rangle = \tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(\|x + y\|^2 - \|x - y\|^2\bigr),
    $$

    and in the complex case the real part combined with the imaginary
    part $\tfrac{1}{4}(\|x + iy\|^2 - \|x - iy\|^2)$ gives a $K$-linear
    inner product.

??? proof "Proof (Norm from a polarisation identity)"
    **($\Leftarrow$)** Assume the norm comes from an inner product. Expand
    $\|x \pm y\|^2 = \|x\|^2 \pm 2 \mathrm{Re}\langle x, y \rangle + \|y\|^2$
    and add: the cross terms cancel and we get $2\|x\|^2 + 2\|y\|^2$.
    **($\Rightarrow$)** Conversely, the real case. Define $\langle x, y
    \rangle$ by the polarisation formula. By symmetry $\langle y, x
    \rangle = \langle x, y \rangle$ and $\langle x, x \rangle = \|x\|^2 \geq
    0$. To check additivity in the first variable, expand $\|x + y +
    z\|^2 + \|x + y - z\|^2$ in two ways (using the parallelogram law
    with $x + y, z$ and with $x, y$ respectively) and take the average;
    the difference gives $\langle x + y, z \rangle = \langle x, z \rangle +
    \langle y, z \rangle$. For scalar multiplication, additivity plus
    $\langle -x, y \rangle = -\langle x, y \rangle$ (check via the
    polarisation) gives $\mathbb{Z}$-linearity; by induction
    $\langle nx, y \rangle = n \langle x, y \rangle$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
    Rationality follows from the previous: $n \langle (p/q) x, y \rangle
    = \langle p x, y \rangle = p \langle x, y \rangle$. By continuity
    (the polarisation shows $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ is continuous
    in each variable) and density of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}$, this
    extends to all $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$.
    For the complex case, define $\langle x, y \rangle_{\mathbb{C}} =
    \tfrac{1}{4}(\|x + y\|^2 - \|x - y\|^2) + \tfrac{i}{4}(\|x + iy\|^2 -
    \|x - iy\|^2)$. The real part is a real inner product by the
    previous case. A direct computation (using the real form on
    $\langle x, iy \rangle$) gives
    $\langle x, y \rangle_{\mathbb{C}} = \langle x, y \rangle_1$ when
    $y \in V$ is real, and $\langle x, iy \rangle_{\mathbb{C}} = i
    \langle x, y \rangle_{\mathbb{C}}$ by a similar expansion. Hence
    $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_{\mathbb{C}}$ is $\mathbb{C}$-linear in
    the second slot, conjugate-symmetric by the real case, and
    positive by the positivity of the real part.

## Orthogonal Projection

!!! definition "Definition (Hilbert space)"
    <a id="def-sa-2-1"></a>
    A complete inner product space is a **Hilbert space**. Any
    finite-dimensional inner product space is automatically a Hilbert
    space.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Existence and uniqueness of orthogonal projection)"
    <a id="thm-sa-2-2"></a>
    Let $(V, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ be an inner product space and
    $W$ a subspace which is a Hilbert space under the restricted inner
    product. For every $x \in V$ there is a unique
    $p_W(x) \in W$ minimising $\|x - y\|$ over $y \in W$, characterised
    by $x - p_W(x) \in W^\perp$.

??? proof "Proof (Existence and uniqueness of orthogonal projection)"
    **Existence.** Let $c = \inf_{y \in W} \|x - y\|$ and choose
    $(y_n) \subset W$ with $\|x - y_n\| \to c$. By the parallelogram
    identity,
    $\|y_n - y_m\|^2 = 2\|y_n - x\|^2 + 2\|y_m - x\|^2 - 4\|
    \tfrac{y_n + y_m}{2} - x\|^2 \leq 2\|y_n - x\|^2 + 2\|y_m - x\|^2 - 4c^2$.
    Hence $(y_n)$ is Cauchy in $W$ and converges (in $W$, by
    completeness) to some $p_W(x) \in W$. Continuity of the norm gives
    $\|x - p_W(x)\| = c$.
    **Orthogonality.** Fix $y \in W$ and pick $a \in K$ with $|a| = 1$
    and $a \langle x - p_W(x), y \rangle = |\langle x - p_W(x), y \rangle|$.
    For any $t \in \mathbb{R}$,
    $\|x - (p_W(x) + t a y)\|^2 = \|x - p_W(x)\|^2 - 2t |\langle x -
    p_W(x), y \rangle| + t^2 \|y\|^2 \geq c^2$. The minimum at $t = 0$
    forces $|\langle x - p_W(x), y \rangle| = 0$, so $x - p_W(x) \in W^\perp$.
    **Uniqueness.** If $z \in W$ with $x - z \in W^\perp$, then
    $\|x - p_W(x)\|^2 = \|x - z\|^2 + \|z - p_W(x)\|^2$ (Pythagoras,
    using $(x - z) \perp (z - p_W(x)) \in W$). So $z = p_W(x)$.

!!! definition "Definition (Orthonormal family, basis)"
    <a id="def-sa-2-3"></a>
    A family $(x_i)_{i \in I}$ of non-zero vectors is **orthogonal** if
    $\langle x_i, x_j \rangle = 0$ for $i \neq j$ and **orthonormal** if
    in addition $\|x_i\| = 1$. An orthogonal (resp. orthonormal) family
    spanning a dense subspace is an **orthogonal (resp. orthonormal)
    basis**.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Linear independence of orthogonal families)"
    <a id="prop-sa-2-4"></a>
    Every orthogonal family in an inner product space is linearly
    independent.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Parseval)"
    <a id="thm-sa-2-5"></a>
    Let $(V, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ be a Hilbert space with
    orthonormal basis $(e_i)_{i \in I}$. For every $x \in V$,

    $$
    \sum_{i \in I} |\langle e_i, x \rangle|^2 \leq \|x\|^2
    $$

    and $p_{\overline{\mathrm{span}}(e_i)}(x) = \sum_i \langle e_i, x
    \rangle e_i$.

??? proof "Proof (Parseval)"
    For a finite $J \subseteq I$, set
    $y = x - \sum_{i \in J} \langle e_i, x \rangle e_i$. For each
    $j \in J$, $\langle y, e_j \rangle = \langle x, e_j \rangle -
    \langle x, e_j \rangle = 0$, so $y \in \mathrm{span}(J)^\perp$.
    By Pythagoras,
    $\|x\|^2 = \|y\|^2 + \sum_{i \in J} |\langle e_i, x \rangle|^2 \geq
    \sum_{i \in J} |\langle e_i, x \rangle|^2$.
    Taking the supremum over all finite $J \subseteq I$ gives Bessel's
    inequality. In particular $\sum_i |\langle e_i, x \rangle|^2 < \infty$
    so the limit $z = \sum_i \langle e_i, x \rangle e_i$ exists in $V$
    (Prop 3.2.6). For any $j \in I$,
    $\langle z, e_j \rangle = \langle x, e_j \rangle$ (the series
    converges absolutely in $\mathbb{C}$), so $z - x \in
    \mathrm{span}(e_i)^\perp = \overline{\mathrm{span}}(e_i)^\perp$.
    Hence $z = p_{\overline{\mathrm{span}}(e_i)}(x)$.

## Adjoint of a Bounded Linear Map

!!! definition "Definition (Bounded linear operator, norm)"
    <a id="def-sa-3-1"></a>
    Let $V, W$ be normed spaces. A linear map $T : V \to W$ is
    **bounded** if

    $$
    \|T\| := \sup_{\|x\| \leq 1} \|T x\|
    $$

    is finite. The space $L(V, W)$ of bounded operators is itself a
    normed space; it is a Banach space when $W$ is.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Riesz representation for Hilbert spaces)"
    <a id="thm-sa-3-2"></a>
    Let $(V, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ be a Hilbert space. The map
    $x \mapsto \ell_x = \langle x, \cdot \rangle$ is a conjugate-linear
    isometric isomorphism $V \to V^\vee$ (the dual of $V$).

    **Consequence.** Every continuous linear functional on a Hilbert
    space has a unique representing vector.

!!! definition "Definition (Adjoint of a bounded operator)"
    <a id="def-sa-3-3"></a>
    Let $V, W$ be Hilbert spaces and $T \in L(V, W)$. The **adjoint**
    $T^* \in L(W, V)$ is defined by
    $\langle T x, y \rangle_W = \langle x, T^* y \rangle_V$ for all
    $x \in V$, $y \in W$. Existence follows from Riesz: $T^*$ is the
    composite $V \xrightarrow{T} W \xrightarrow{(\cdot)^\vee} W^\vee
    \xrightarrow{\cong} V$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Properties of the adjoint)"
    <a id="prop-sa-3-4"></a>
    1. $(T + S)^* = T^* + S^*$, $(\lambda T)^* = \overline{\lambda}\,
       T^*$, $\mathrm{Id}^* = \mathrm{Id}$.
    2. $(T \circ S)^* = S^* \circ T^*$.
    3. $\|T^*\| = \|T\|$ and $\|T^* T\| = \|T\|^2$.
    4. $(T^*)^* = T$.

??? proof "Proof (Properties of the adjoint)"
    1. $\langle (T + S) x, y \rangle = \langle Tx, y \rangle +
       \langle Sx, y \rangle = \langle x, T^* y \rangle + \langle x,
       S^* y \rangle = \langle x, (T^* + S^*) y \rangle$, so
       $(T + S)^* = T^* + S^*$. For $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$,
       $\langle (\lambda T) x, y \rangle = \lambda \langle Tx, y \rangle
       = \lambda \langle x, T^* y \rangle = \langle x, \overline{\lambda}
       T^* y \rangle$, so $(\lambda T)^* = \overline{\lambda} T^*$.
       $\mathrm{Id}^*$ is the identity by definition.
    2. $\langle (TS) x, y \rangle = \langle Sx, T^* y \rangle
       = \langle x, S^* T^* y \rangle$, so $(TS)^* = S^* T^*$.
    3. $\|T^*\| = \sup_{\|y\| \leq 1} \sup_{\|x\| \leq 1}
       |\langle Tx, y \rangle| = \sup_{\|x\| \leq 1} \|Tx\| = \|T\|$.
       For the second equality, $\|T^* T\| \leq \|T^*\| \|T\| = \|T\|^2$.
       Conversely, $\|T x\|^2 = \langle Tx, Tx \rangle = \langle x,
       T^* T x \rangle \leq \|T^* T\| \|x\|^2$, so $\|T\|^2 \leq
       \|T^* T\|$.
    4. Conjugate the definition: $\langle y, T^{**} x \rangle
       = \overline{\langle x, T^* y \rangle} = \overline{\langle Tx, y
       \rangle} = \langle y, Tx \rangle$ for all $x, y$, so $T^{**} = T$.

## Reduction of Self-adjoint Maps

!!! definition "Definition (Self-adjoint, positive, unitary)"
    <a id="def-sa-4-1"></a>
    A bounded operator $T \in L(V)$ on a Hilbert space is

    + **self-adjoint** (Hermitian) if $T^* = T$;
    + **positive** if $T = T^*$ and $\langle T x, x \rangle \geq 0$ for
      all $x$;
    + **unitary** if $T^* T = T T^* = \mathrm{Id}_V$;
    + **normal** if $T T^* = T^* T$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators)"
    <a id="thm-sa-4-2"></a>
    Let $V$ be a (real) Hilbert space and $T = T^* \in L(V)$ a
    self-adjoint bounded operator. Then there is a direct-sum
    decomposition $V = \bigoplus_{\lambda \in \mathrm{sp}(T)} V_\lambda$
    into $T$-invariant subspaces on which $T$ acts by the scalar
    $\lambda$ (the **spectral decomposition**). In the complex case the
    same holds for normal operators.

    The spectrum $\mathrm{sp}(T) = \{\lambda \in K : T - \lambda
    \mathrm{Id} \text{ is not invertible}\}$ is a non-empty compact
    subset of $K$.

??? proof "Proof (Sketch of the reduction)"
    Use the existence of a cyclic vector for $T$ (functional calculus
    via the Weierstrass approximation theorem) and the Gelfand–Naimark
    representation of the $C^*$-algebra generated by $T$. In the finite
    rank case one shows that $V$ splits as a direct sum of eigenspaces
    by induction on dimension.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Spectral mapping)"
    <a id="prop-sa-4-3"></a>
    If $T$ is self-adjoint with spectral decomposition $V =
    \bigoplus_\lambda V_\lambda$ and $p \in K[T]$, then
    $p(T) = \bigoplus_\lambda p(\lambda) \mathrm{Id}_{V_\lambda}$ and
    $T$ is **positive** iff $\lambda \geq 0$ for every eigenvalue.

??? proof "Proof (Spectral mapping)"
    On each eigenspace $V_\lambda$, $T|_V = \lambda \mathrm{Id}$, so
    $T^k|_V = \lambda^k \mathrm{Id}$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}$. By
    linearity, $p(T)|_{V_\lambda} = p(\lambda) \mathrm{Id}_{V_\lambda}$
    for any polynomial $p$. So $p(T) = \bigoplus_\lambda p(\lambda)
    \mathrm{Id}_{V_\lambda}$.
    For $T$ positive, $\langle T v, v \rangle \geq 0$ for all $v$. Take
    $v \in V_\lambda \setminus \{0\}$: $\langle Tv, v \rangle =
    \lambda \|v\|^2 \geq 0$, so $\lambda \geq 0$. Conversely, if
    $\lambda \geq 0$ for all eigenvalues, then
    $\langle Tv, v \rangle = \sum_\lambda \lambda \|v_\lambda\|^2 \geq 0$
    for any $v = \sum v_\lambda$ (decompose $v$ according to the
    spectral decomposition).

## Linear Isometries

!!! definition "Definition (Linear isometry)"
    <a id="def-sa-5-1"></a>
    A bounded operator $U : V \to W$ between Hilbert spaces is a
    **linear isometry** if $\|U x\| = \|x\|$ for all $x \in V$. It is
    **coisometric** if $U^*$ is an isometry, and **unitary** if both
    (equivalently, $U^* U = \mathrm{Id}_V$ and $U U^* = \mathrm{Id}_W$).

!!! proposition "Proposition (Characterisations of isometries)"
    <a id="prop-sa-5-2"></a>
    For $U \in L(V, W)$ the following are equivalent:

    1. $U$ is a linear isometry.
    2. $\langle U x, U y \rangle_W = \langle x, y \rangle_V$ for all
       $x, y$.
    3. $U^* U = \mathrm{Id}_V$.

    In particular, isometries are automatically injective, and a
    surjective isometry is unitary.

??? proof "Proof (Characterisations of isometries)"
    (1 $\Rightarrow$ 2): $\|Ux\|^2 = \langle Ux, Ux \rangle = \|x\|^2
    = \langle x, x \rangle$ for all $x$. Polarise:
    $\langle Ux, Uy \rangle = \tfrac{1}{4}(\|U(x+y)\|^2 - \|U(x-y)\|^2
    + i\|U(x+iy)\|^2 - i\|U(x-iy)\|^2)
    = \tfrac{1}{4}(\|x+y\|^2 - \|x-y\|^2 + i\|x+iy\|^2 - i\|x-iy\|^2)
    = \langle x, y \rangle$.
    (2 $\Rightarrow$ 3): $\langle x, y \rangle = \langle Ux, Uy \rangle
    = \langle x, U^* U y \rangle$ for all $x, y$, so
    $U^* U = \mathrm{Id}_V$.
    (3 $\Rightarrow$ 1): $\|Ux\|^2 = \langle Ux, Ux \rangle
    = \langle x, U^* U x \rangle = \langle x, x \rangle = \|x\|^2$.
    The last remark: (3) gives injectivity of $U$; surjectivity adds
    $U U^* = \mathrm{Id}_W$ by the symmetric argument.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Singular value decomposition)"
    <a id="thm-sa-5-3"></a>
    Every bounded operator $T \in L(V, W)$ admits a factorisation
    $T = U \Sigma V^*$ with $V \in L(V)$, $U \in L(W)$ unitary and
    $\Sigma \in L(V, W)$ diagonal with non-negative entries
    $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq 0$ (the **singular values**).

    **Consequence.** $T$ is compact iff its sequence of singular values
    is square-summable. $\|T\| = \sigma_1$ and
    $\mathrm{sp}(T^* T) = \{\sigma_i^2\}$.

??? proof "Proof (Singular value decomposition, sketch)"
    $T^* T \in L(V)$ is self-adjoint and non-negative (by Cauchy–
    Schwarz, $\langle T^* T v, v \rangle = \|Tv\|^2 \geq 0$). By the
    spectral theorem, $T^* T$ has an orthonormal eigenbasis $(e_i)$
    with eigenvalues $\sigma_i^2 \geq 0$. Define $V$ as the unitary
    operator mapping the $i$-th standard basis vector to $e_i$.
    On each $V_\lambda$ (eigenspace for eigenvalue $\lambda = \sigma_i^2$),
    set $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda}$ and $\Sigma e_i = \sigma_i e_i$.
    For $\sigma_i \neq 0$, $U(\sigma_i e_i) := T e_i$ extends by
    orthonormality to a unitary $U$ from $\overline{\mathrm{Im}\, \Sigma}$
    to $\overline{\mathrm{Im}\, T}$. Then $T = U \Sigma V^*$.

    **Consequences.** $T$ compact iff the sequence of singular values
    $(\sigma_i)$ is square-summable (the spectral theorem applied to
    $T^* T$). $\|T\| = \sigma_1$ (largest eigenvalue of $(T^* T)^{1/2}$)
    and $\mathrm{sp}(T^* T) = \{\sigma_i^2\}$.

!!! example "Example (Polar decomposition)"
    Any $T \in L(V)$ admits a unique factorisation $T = UP$ with
    $U$ unitary on $\overline{\mathrm{Im}\, T}$ and $P = (T^* T)^{1/2}$
    positive. When $T$ is invertible, $U$ is unitary.
