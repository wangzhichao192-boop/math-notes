# Self-adjoint Operators

This chapter develops the **geometry of inner-product spaces** and the
**operator theory of bounded self-adjoint linear maps** on a Hilbert space.
The cornerstone is the **orthogonal projection** onto a complete subspace,
which exists in any Hilbert space by the parallelogram identity. We then
study **adjoints** of bounded operators and the **spectral reduction** of
self-adjoint maps.

## Inner Product Spaces

!!! definition "Definition (Semi-inner / inner product)"
    <a id="def-sa-1-1"></a>
    <a id="def-semi-inner-inner-product"></a>

    A map $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : V \times V \to K$ on a vector
    space $V$ over $K \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}$ is a **semi-inner
    product** if

    1. $\langle x, \cdot \rangle$ is linear for every $x$;
    2. it is **conjugate symmetric**:
       $\langle y, x \rangle = \overline{\langle x, y \rangle}$;
    3. it is **semi-positive**: $\langle x, x \rangle \geq 0$.

    It is an **inner product** if additionally $\langle x, x \rangle > 0$
    for all $x \neq 0$. $(V, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ is then an
    **inner product space**.

!!! definition "Definition (Orthogonality, orthogonal complement)"
    <a id="def-sa-1-2"></a>
    <a id="def-orthogonality-orthogonal-complement"></a>

    In a semi-inner product space, $x \perp y$ iff $\langle x, y \rangle = 0$. The **orthogonal complement** of $A \subseteq V$ is
    $A^\perp = \{y : \forall x \in A,\ \langle x, y \rangle = 0\}$, a
    vector subspace. The double orthogonal complement contains the subspace generated by $A$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Properties of the orthogonal complement)"
    <a id="prop-sa-1-3"></a>

    <a id="prop-properties-of-the-orthogonal-complement"></a>

    1. $A^\perp$ is a subspace; if $A \subseteq B$ then $B^\perp \subseteq A^\perp$.

    2. The subspace generated by $A$ is contained in $(A^\perp)^\perp$.

    3. If $W$ is the subspace generated by $A$, then $A^\perp = W^\perp$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Cauchy–Schwarz)"
    <a id="thm-sa-1-4"></a>
    <a id="thm-cauchyschwarz"></a>

    For any $(x, y) \in V \times V$ in a semi-inner product space,
    $|\langle x, y \rangle|^2 \leq \langle x, x \rangle \langle y, y \rangle$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Choose $u$ with $|u|=1$ and $u\langle x,y\rangle=|\langle x,y\rangle|$. For every real $t$, positivity gives $0\leq\|x\|^2+2t|\langle x,y\rangle|+t^2\|y\|^2$. If $\|y\|=0$, this affine expression can be nonnegative for all $t$ only if its linear coefficient vanishes. Otherwise its discriminant is nonpositive, giving the inequality. Both cases are thereby covered.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Norm from an inner product)"
    <a id="prop-sa-1-5"></a>
    <a id="prop-norm-from-an-inner-product"></a>

    The map $\|x\| := \sqrt{\langle x, x \rangle}$ is a seminorm on $V$,
    and a norm iff $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ is an inner product.
    The kernel of $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ equals $\{x : \|x\| = 0\}$.
    For any subspace $W$ of the kernel, the quotient
    $(V / W, \langle [\cdot], [\cdot] \rangle^\sim)$ is a semi-inner
    product space with $\langle [x], [y] \rangle^\sim = \langle x, y \rangle$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Bounded evaluation)"
    <a id="prop-sa-1-6"></a>
    <a id="prop-bounded-evaluation"></a>

    For an inner product space $V$, the linear form
    $\ell_x : V \to K$, $z \mapsto \langle x, z \rangle$ is bounded with
    $\|\ell_x\| = \|x\|$. In particular, $\ell_x = \ell_y$ iff $x = y$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Norm from a polarisation identity)"
    <a id="prop-sa-1-7"></a>
    <a id="prop-norm-from-a-polarisation-identity"></a>

    A norm $\|\cdot\|$ on a real (resp. complex) vector space is induced
    by an inner product iff the parallelogram identity

    $$
    \|x + y\|^2 + \|x - y\|^2 = 2 \|x\|^2 + 2 \|y\|^2
    $$

    holds for all $x, y$. In the real case the polarisation is

    $$
    \langle x, y \rangle = \tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(\|x + y\|^2 - \|x - y\|^2\bigr),
    $$

    and in the complex case the real part combined with the imaginary
    part $-\tfrac{1}{4}(\|x + iy\|^2 - \|x - iy\|^2)$ gives a $K$-linear
    inner product.

??? proof "Proof"
    An inner product implies the parallelogram identity by expanding both squares. Conversely put $Q(x)=\|x\|^2$ and $B(x,y)=(Q(x+y)-Q(x-y))/4$. It is symmetric and continuous. The parallelogram identity gives $B(x+z,y)+B(x-z,y)=2B(x,y)$ by pairing the two positive and the two negative terms. Setting $z=x$ gives $B(2x,y)=2B(x,y)$, since $B(0,y)=0$. Now replace $x,z$ by $(u+v)/2,(u-v)/2$ to obtain $B(u,y)+B(v,y)=B(u+v,y)$. Symmetry gives additivity in both slots. Integer and then rational homogeneity follow by addition and division; continuity and density of rationals give real bilinearity. Also $B(x,x)=Q(x)$, so this is the required real inner product.

    For $K=\mathbb C$, scalar norm homogeneity gives $B(ix,iy)=B(x,y)$ and hence $B(ix,y)=-B(x,iy)$. Define $H(x,y)=B(x,y)-iB(x,iy)$. Then $H(x,iy)=iH(x,y)$, so real bilinearity makes the second slot complex linear. The same identities give conjugate symmetry. Finally $B(x,ix)=0$ and $H(x,x)=Q(x)$, proving positivity. Thus the imaginary term has a minus sign under the second-slot-linear convention.

!!! example "Example (Canonical inner product and conjugate linearity)"
    <a id="ex-canonical-inner-product-and-conjugate-linearity"></a>

    Throughout this chapter $K=\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$, and the second slot is linear. Thus $\langle ax+by,z\rangle=\bar a\langle x,z\rangle+\bar b\langle y,z\rangle$. The canonical product on $K^n$ is $\sum_i\bar x_iy_i$. Orthogonality is symmetric, is preserved by scalar multiples, and gives the Pythagorean identity $\|x+y\|^2=\|x\|^2+\|y\|^2$.

??? proof "Proof"
    The orthogonal-complement properties follow by conjugate linearity, conjugate symmetry and inclusion of the defining conditions. Orthogonality to a generating set is equivalent to orthogonality to every finite linear combination of it. The triangle inequality for the associated seminorm follows from $\|x+y\|^2=\|x\|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\langle x,y\rangle+\|y\|^2\leq(\|x\|+\|y\|)^2$. Homogeneity follows from $\langle ax,ax\rangle=|a|^2\langle x,x\rangle$. Cauchy–Schwarz identifies the zero-seminorm vectors with the kernel. Quotienting by a subspace of the kernel is well defined because all four cross terms involving that subspace vanish. Quotienting by the full kernel gives a positive definite inner product.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Closed orthogonal complements)"
    <a id="closed-orthogonal-complement"></a>

    Every orthogonal complement in an inner product space is closed, and $A^\perp=(\overline A)^\perp$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Cauchy–Schwarz bounds $\ell_x(y)=\langle x,y\rangle$ by $\|x\|\|y\|$, while evaluation at $y=x/\|x\|$ gives $\|\ell_x\|=\|x\|$ for $x\neq0$. The zero case is immediate. Thus $A^\perp=\bigcap_{x\in A}\ker\ell_x$ is closed. If $y$ is orthogonal to $A$, the closed set $\ker\ell_y$ contains $A$ and hence its closure, proving the second assertion. Also $\|\ell_x-\ell_y\|=\|x-y\|$, which gives uniqueness of representing vectors.

## Orthogonal Projection

!!! definition "Definition (Hilbert space)"
    <a id="def-sa-2-1"></a>
    <a id="def-hilbert-space"></a>

    A complete inner product space is a **Hilbert space**. Any
    finite-dimensional inner product space is automatically a Hilbert
    space.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Existence and uniqueness of orthogonal projection)"
    <a id="thm-sa-2-2"></a>
    <a id="thm-existence-and-uniqueness-of-orthogonal-projection"></a>

    Let $(V, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ be an inner product space and
    $W$ a subspace which is a Hilbert space under the restricted inner
    product. For every $x \in V$ there is a unique
    $p_W(x) \in W$ minimising $\|x - y\|$ over $y \in W$, characterised
    by $x - p_W(x) \in W^\perp$.

??? proof "Proof"
    **Existence.** Let $c = \inf_{y \in W} \|x - y\|$ and choose
    $(y_n) \subset W$ with $\|x - y_n\| \to c$. By the parallelogram
    identity,
    $\|y_n - y_m\|^2 = 2\|y_n - x\|^2 + 2\|y_m - x\|^2 - 4\| \tfrac{y_n + y_m}{2} - x\|^2 \leq 2\|y_n - x\|^2 + 2\|y_m - x\|^2 - 4c^2$.
    Hence $(y_n)$ is Cauchy in $W$ and converges (in $W$, by
    completeness) to some $p_W(x) \in W$. Continuity of the norm gives
    $\|x - p_W(x)\| = c$.
    **Orthogonality.** Fix $y \in W$ and pick $a \in K$ with $|a| = 1$
    and $a \langle x - p_W(x), y \rangle = |\langle x - p_W(x), y \rangle|$.
    For any $t \in \mathbb{R}$,
    $\|x - (p_W(x) + t a y)\|^2 = \|x - p_W(x)\|^2 - 2t |\langle x - p_W(x), y \rangle| + t^2 \|y\|^2 \geq c^2$. The minimum at $t = 0$
    forces $|\langle x - p_W(x), y \rangle| = 0$, so $x - p_W(x) \in W^\perp$.
    **Uniqueness.** If $z \in W$ with $x - z \in W^\perp$, then
    $\|x - p_W(x)\|^2 = \|x - z\|^2 + \|z - p_W(x)\|^2$ (Pythagoras,
    using $(x - z) \perp (z - p_W(x)) \in W$). So $z = p_W(x)$.

!!! definition "Definition (Orthonormal family, basis)"
    <a id="def-sa-2-3"></a>
    <a id="def-orthonormal-family-basis"></a>

    A family $(x_i)_{i \in I}$ of non-zero vectors is **orthogonal** if
    $\langle x_i, x_j \rangle = 0$ for $i \neq j$ and **orthonormal** if
    in addition $\|x_i\| = 1$. An orthogonal (resp. orthonormal) family
    spanning a dense subspace is an **orthogonal (resp. orthonormal)
    basis**.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Linear independence of orthogonal families)"
    <a id="prop-sa-2-4"></a>
    <a id="prop-linear-independence-of-orthogonal-families"></a>

    Every orthogonal family in an inner product space is linearly
    independent.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Parseval)"
    <a id="thm-sa-2-5"></a>
    <a id="thm-parseval"></a>

    Let $(V, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ be a Hilbert space with
    orthonormal basis $(e_i)_{i \in I}$. For every $x \in V$,

    $$
    \sum_{i \in I} |\langle e_i, x \rangle|^2 \leq \|x\|^2
    $$

    and $p_{\overline{\mathrm{span}}(e_i)}(x) = \sum_i \langle e_i, x \rangle e_i$.

??? proof "Proof"
    For a finite $J \subseteq I$, set
    $y = x - \sum_{i \in J} \langle e_i, x \rangle e_i$. For each
    $j \in J$, $\langle y, e_j \rangle = \langle x, e_j \rangle - \langle x, e_j \rangle = 0$, so $y \in \mathrm{span}(J)^\perp$.
    By Pythagoras,
    $\|x\|^2 = \|y\|^2 + \sum_{i \in J} |\langle e_i, x \rangle|^2 \geq \sum_{i \in J} |\langle e_i, x \rangle|^2$.
    Taking the supremum over all finite $J \subseteq I$ gives Bessel's
    inequality. In particular $\sum_i |\langle e_i, x \rangle|^2 < \infty$
    so the limit $z = \sum_i \langle e_i, x \rangle e_i$ exists in $V$
    ([Proposition](#summable-orthogonal-family)). For any $j \in I$,
    $\langle z, e_j \rangle = \langle x, e_j \rangle$ (the series
    converges absolutely in $\mathbb{C}$), so $z - x \in \mathrm{span}(e_i)^\perp = \overline{\mathrm{span}}(e_i)^\perp$.
    Hence $z = p_{\overline{\mathrm{span}}(e_i)}(x)$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Summing an arbitrary orthogonal family)"
    <a id="summable-orthogonal-family"></a>

    In a Hilbert space, if $x_i\neq0$ are pairwise orthogonal and $\sum_i|a_i|^2\|x_i\|^2<\infty$, only countably many $a_i$ are nonzero. The sum $\sum_i a_ix_i$ converges independently of their enumeration and satisfies

    $$
    \left\|\sum_i a_ix_i\right\|^2=\sum_i|a_i|^2\|x_i\|^2.
    $$

    Pairing this sum with any fixed $y$ may be done termwise, and the scalar series converges absolutely.

??? proof "Proof"
    For each rational $\varepsilon>0$, there are only finitely many indices with $|a_i|\|x_i\|>\varepsilon$, or the sum of squares would be infinite. Their countable union contains the support. In any enumeration, the squared norm of a finite tail is exactly its sum of squares by Pythagoras. Hence the partial sums are Cauchy and converge by completeness. Their squared error is the remaining square sum. Given two enumerations, place any finite initial set from one inside a finite initial set of the other; the same tail bound proves equality of the limits. Continuity of the norm gives the norm identity.

    Continuity of pairing gives the scalar limit. For absolute convergence choose unit scalars $b_i$ so that $\langle b_ia_ix_i,y\rangle=|\langle a_ix_i,y\rangle|$. The family with coefficients $b_ia_i$ has the same finite square sum and therefore converges. Pairing its sum with $y$ shows that the sum of these nonnegative scalar terms is finite.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Projection, Bessel and Parseval)"
    <a id="projection-bessel-parseval"></a>

    The projection onto a complete subspace $W$ is linear, fixes $W$, satisfies $p_W^2=p_W$, and has norm at most one. For an orthonormal basis $(e_i)$ of $W$, $p_Wx=\sum_i\langle e_i,x\rangle e_i$ and $\sum_i|\langle e_i,x\rangle|^2\leq\|x\|^2$. When $W=V$ this is equality and the expansion equals $x$.

??? proof "Proof"
    A linear combination of the projected vectors lies in $W$ and its residual is orthogonal to $W$, so uniqueness of projection proves linearity. A vector already in $W$ has zero residual, proving the identity and idempotence. Pythagoras bounds $\|p_Wx\|$ by $\|x\|$. Finite partial orthonormal expansions have orthogonal residuals, proving Bessel by taking the supremum of their squared coefficient sums. The preceding orthogonal summation theorem constructs the infinite expansion. Its residual is orthogonal to every $e_i$ and hence to their dense span $W$, identifying it with the projection.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Gram–Schmidt along a flag)"
    <a id="gram-schmidt-flag"></a>

    If $0=V_0\subset V_1\subset\cdots\subset V_n$ with $\dim V_i=i$, there is an orthonormal family $x_i\in V_i\setminus V_{i-1}$ adapted to this flag. For $y_i\in V_i\setminus V_{i-1}$ set

    $$
    z_i=y_i-\sum_{j<i}\langle x_j,y_i\rangle x_j
       =y_i-\sum_{j<i}\frac{\langle z_j,y_i\rangle}{\|z_j\|^2}z_j,
    \qquad x_i=z_i/\|z_i\|.
    $$

    The remainder $z_i$ is nonzero and orthogonal to $V_{i-1}$ by projection, proving the construction. Pythagoras also gives $\|z_i\|^2=\|y_i\|^2-\sum_{j<i}|\langle x_j,y_i\rangle|^2$.

## Adjoint of a Bounded Linear Map

!!! definition "Definition (Bounded linear operator, norm)"
    <a id="def-sa-3-1"></a>
    <a id="def-bounded-linear-operator-norm"></a>

    Let $V, W$ be normed spaces. A linear map $T : V \to W$ is
    **bounded** if

    $$
    \|T\| := \sup_{\|x\| \leq 1} \|T x\|
    $$

    is finite. The space $L(V, W)$ of bounded operators is itself a
    normed space; it is a Banach space when $W$ is.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Riesz representation for Hilbert spaces)"
    <a id="thm-sa-3-2"></a>
    <a id="thm-riesz-representation-for-hilbert-spaces"></a>

    Let $(V, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ be a Hilbert space. The map
    $x \mapsto \ell_x = \langle x, \cdot \rangle$ is a conjugate-linear
    isometric isomorphism $V\to L(V,K)$ (the continuous dual of $V$).

??? proof "Proof"
    The map $x\mapsto\ell_x=\langle x,\cdot\rangle$ is conjugate linear and isometric by bounded evaluation, hence injective. For a nonzero bounded functional $\psi$, its kernel $W$ is a proper closed subspace. The preceding projection lemma supplies a unit $v\in W^\perp$, and $\alpha=\psi(v)\neq0$, since otherwise $v\in W\cap W^\perp$. Each $z$ decomposes as $w+(\psi(z)/\alpha)v$ with $w\in W$. Put $x=\bar\alpha v$. Then $\langle x,z\rangle=\alpha\langle v,z\rangle=\alpha\psi(z)/\alpha=\psi(z)$. The zero functional is represented by zero. This proves surjectivity and uniqueness without circular use of Riesz.

!!! definition "Definition (Adjoint of a bounded operator)"
    <a id="def-sa-3-3"></a>
    <a id="def-adjoint-of-a-bounded-operator"></a>

    Let $V, W$ be Hilbert spaces and $T \in L(V, W)$. The **adjoint**
    $T^* \in L(W, V)$ is defined by
    $\langle T x, y \rangle_W = \langle x, T^* y \rangle_V$ for all
    $x \in V$, $y \in W$. For $y\in W$, apply Riesz to the bounded functional $x\mapsto\langle y,Tx\rangle$ to obtain $T^*y$. The conjugate-linear Riesz identifications on both spaces ensure that $T^*$ is linear; $\|T^*y\|\leq\|T\|\|y\|$ ensures boundedness.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Properties of the adjoint)"
    <a id="prop-sa-3-4"></a>

    <a id="prop-properties-of-the-adjoint"></a>

    1. $(T + S)^* = T^* + S^*$, $(\lambda T)^* = \overline{\lambda}\, T^*$, $\mathrm{Id}^* = \mathrm{Id}$.

    2. $(T \circ S)^* = S^* \circ T^*$.

    3. $\|T^*\| = \|T\|$ and $\|T^* T\| = \|T\|^2$.

    4. $(T^*)^* = T$.

??? proof "Proof"
    1. $\langle (T + S) x, y \rangle = \langle Tx, y \rangle + \langle Sx, y \rangle = \langle x, T^* y \rangle + \langle x, S^* y \rangle = \langle x, (T^* + S^*) y \rangle$, so
       $(T + S)^* = T^* + S^*$. For $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$,
       $\langle (\lambda T) x, y \rangle = \overline\lambda \langle Tx, y \rangle = \overline\lambda \langle x, T^* y \rangle = \langle x, \overline{\lambda} T^* y \rangle$, so $(\lambda T)^* = \overline{\lambda} T^*$.
       $\mathrm{Id}^*$ is the identity by definition.
    2. $\langle (TS) x, y \rangle = \langle Sx, T^* y \rangle = \langle x, S^* T^* y \rangle$, so $(TS)^* = S^* T^*$.
    3. $\|T^*\| = \sup_{\|y\| \leq 1} \sup_{\|x\| \leq 1} |\langle Tx, y \rangle| = \sup_{\|x\| \leq 1} \|Tx\| = \|T\|$.
       For the second equality, $\|T^* T\| \leq \|T^*\| \|T\| = \|T\|^2$.
       Conversely, $\|T x\|^2 = \langle Tx, Tx \rangle = \langle x, T^* T x \rangle \leq \|T^* T\| \|x\|^2$, so $\|T\|^2 \leq \|T^* T\|$.
    4. Conjugate the definition: $\langle y, T^{**} x \rangle = \overline{\langle x, T^* y \rangle} = \overline{\langle Tx, y \rangle} = \langle y, Tx \rangle$ for all $x, y$, so $T^{**} = T$.

!!! lemma "Lemma (A nondense subspace has a nonzero orthogonal complement)"
    <a id="lem-a-nondense-subspace-has-a-nonzero-orthogonal-complement"></a>

    Replace the subspace by its closure. For $x$ outside that proper closed subspace, $x-p_Wx$ is a nonzero orthogonal vector. This establishes the lemma without using Riesz representation.

!!! definition "Definition (Conjugate-linear mapping)"
    <a id="def-conjugate-linear-mapping"></a>

    A conjugate-linear mapping satisfies $f(ax+by)=\bar af(x)+\bar bf(y)$. A bijective one is a conjugate-linear isomorphism. Over $\mathbb R$ these are simply linear mappings and isomorphisms.

!!! example "Example (Adjoint matrices and projections)"
    <a id="ex-adjoint-matrices-and-projections"></a>

    In orthonormal coordinates the adjoint matrix is the conjugate transpose $A^*=\overline A^T$; for real matrices it is the transpose. A self-adjoint real matrix is symmetric, and a self-adjoint complex matrix is Hermitian. A projection $p_W$ viewed as an endomorphism is self-adjoint, since $\langle x,p_Wy\rangle=\langle p_Wx,p_Wy\rangle=\langle p_Wx,y\rangle$.

## Reduction of Self-adjoint Maps

!!! definition "Definition (Self-adjoint, positive, unitary)"
    <a id="def-sa-4-1"></a>
    <a id="def-self-adjoint-positive-unitary"></a>

    A bounded operator $T \in L(V)$ on a Hilbert space is

    + **self-adjoint** (Hermitian) if $T^* = T$;
    + **positive** if $T = T^*$ and $\langle T x, x \rangle \geq 0$ for
      all $x$;
    + **unitary** if $T^* T = T T^* = \mathrm{Id}_V$;
    + **normal** if $T T^* = T^* T$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators)"
    <a id="thm-sa-4-2"></a>
    <a id="thm-spectral-theorem-for-self-adjoint-operators"></a>

    A self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional real or complex inner product space has an orthonormal eigenbasis, all of whose eigenvalues are real. Thus $V=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\operatorname{sp}(T)}\ker(T-\lambda I)$. In nonzero finite dimension the spectrum is the nonempty finite set of eigenvalues and hence compact.

    For a finite-dimensional complex normal operator an orthonormal eigenbasis also exists: choose an eigenvalue by the fundamental theorem of algebra, use $\|(T-\lambda I)^*x\|=\|(T-\lambda I)x\|$ to identify the corresponding adjoint eigenvector, and induct on the invariant orthogonal complement. An infinite-dimensional self-adjoint operator need not have an eigenbasis; the finite-dimensional decomposition asserted here has that essential hypothesis.

??? proof "Proof"
    Every eigenvalue of a self-adjoint operator is real, because $\langle v,Tv\rangle=\langle Tv,v\rangle$ and $\|v\|^2>0$. To find an eigenvector without assuming one exists, maximize $q(x)=\langle x,Tx\rangle$ on the compact unit sphere in finite dimension. Let $e$ be a unit maximizing vector and $\lambda=q(e)$. Homogeneity yields $q(z)\leq\lambda\|z\|^2$ for all $z$.

    For arbitrary $y$ and unit scalar $\theta$, substitute $z=\theta e+ty$ and compare the coefficients to first order as $t\downarrow0$. The result is $\operatorname{Re}(\theta\langle y,Te-\lambda e\rangle)\leq0$. Replace $y$ by $-y$ to obtain equality. Choosing the phase $\theta$ gives $\langle y,Te-\lambda e\rangle=0$ for every $y$, hence $Te=\lambda e$. Self-adjointness makes $e^\perp$ invariant: $\langle e,Ty\rangle=\langle Te,y\rangle=0$ for $y\perp e$. Its restriction is self-adjoint. Induction on dimension gives an orthonormal eigenbasis of that complement and then of the whole space. The zero-dimensional case is immediate.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Spectral mapping)"
    <a id="prop-sa-4-3"></a>
    <a id="prop-spectral-mapping"></a>

    In finite dimension, if $T$ is self-adjoint with spectral decomposition $V = \bigoplus_\lambda V_\lambda$ and $p \in K[T]$, then
    $p(T) = \bigoplus_\lambda p(\lambda) \mathrm{Id}_{V_\lambda}$ and
    $T$ is **positive** iff $\lambda \geq 0$ for every eigenvalue.

??? proof "Proof"
    On each eigenspace $V_\lambda$, $T|_V = \lambda \mathrm{Id}$, so
    $T^k|_V = \lambda^k \mathrm{Id}$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}$. By
    linearity, $p(T)|_{V_\lambda} = p(\lambda) \mathrm{Id}_{V_\lambda}$
    for any polynomial $p$. So $p(T) = \bigoplus_\lambda p(\lambda) \mathrm{Id}_{V_\lambda}$.
    For $T$ positive, $\langle T v, v \rangle \geq 0$ for all $v$. Take
    $v \in V_\lambda \setminus \{0\}$: $\langle Tv, v \rangle = \lambda \|v\|^2 \geq 0$, so $\lambda \geq 0$. Conversely, if
    $\lambda \geq 0$ for all eigenvalues, then
    $\langle Tv, v \rangle = \sum_\lambda \lambda \|v_\lambda\|^2 \geq 0$
    for any $v = \sum v_\lambda$ (decompose $v$ according to the
    spectral decomposition).

!!! definition "Definition (Eigenvalues, diagonalization and resolvent)"
    <a id="eigenvalue-resolvent"></a>

    A scalar $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue when $\ker(T-\lambda I)\neq0$; a nonzero vector in this kernel is an eigenvector. Diagonalization means choosing a basis of eigenvectors, so the coordinate operator is $\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$.

    On a Banach space a regular scalar makes $\lambda I-T$ bijective; the bounded inverse theorem then makes its inverse bounded. The set of regular scalars is the resolvent and its complement is the spectrum. The source denotes the resolvent by $\sigma$ and the spectrum by $\rho$, reversing the common convention. Here $\operatorname{sp}(T)$ denotes the spectrum unambiguously. In finite dimension its points are exactly the eigenvalues; in infinite dimension an injective nonsurjective operator also contributes a spectral point.

!!! example "Example (A spectral point that is not an eigenvalue)"
    <a id="ex-a-spectral-point-that-is-not-an-eigenvalue"></a>

    On a Hilbert space with orthonormal basis $e_n$, $n\geq1$, define $Te_n=e_n/n$. It is bounded and injective. Its inverse on its image sends $e_n$ to $ne_n$ and is unbounded. If $T$ were surjective, bounded inversion would contradict this estimate. Thus zero lies in the spectrum but is not an eigenvalue. Its nonzero eigenvalues are exactly $1/n$, as the coordinate equation $(1/n-\lambda)a_n=0$ shows.

!!! corollary "Corollary (Norm and signs of a finite-dimensional self-adjoint operator)"
    <a id="self-adjoint-norm-signs"></a>

    For nonzero finite-dimensional $V$, $\|T\|=\max_i|\lambda_i|$. Positive or negative semidefiniteness is equivalent to all eigenvalues being respectively nonnegative or nonpositive; definiteness corresponds to strict inequalities.

??? proof "Proof"
    In an orthonormal eigenbasis, $\|Tx\|^2=\sum_i|\lambda_i|^2|a_i|^2\leq(\max_i|\lambda_i|)^2\|x\|^2$. Equality in the operator bound occurs at a maximizing eigenvector. Also $\langle x,Tx\rangle=\sum_i\lambda_i|a_i|^2$; testing basis vectors gives necessity of each sign criterion and the sum gives sufficiency. The norm of the zero-dimensional operator is zero.

## Linear Isometries

!!! definition "Definition (Linear isometry)"
    <a id="def-sa-5-1"></a>
    <a id="def-linear-isometry"></a>

    A bounded operator $U : V \to W$ between Hilbert spaces is a
    **linear isometry** if $\|U x\| = \|x\|$ for all $x \in V$. It is
    **coisometric** if $U^*$ is an isometry, and **unitary** if both
    (equivalently, $U^* U = \mathrm{Id}_V$ and $U U^* = \mathrm{Id}_W$).

!!! proposition "Proposition (Characterisations of isometries)"
    <a id="prop-sa-5-2"></a>
    <a id="prop-characterisations-of-isometries"></a>

    For $U \in L(V, W)$ the following are equivalent:

    1. $U$ is a linear isometry.
    2. $\langle U x, U y \rangle_W = \langle x, y \rangle_V$ for all
       $x, y$.
    3. $U^* U = \mathrm{Id}_V$.

    In particular, isometries are automatically injective, and a
    surjective isometry is unitary.

??? proof "Proof"
    (1 $\Rightarrow$ 2): $\|Ux\|^2 = \langle Ux, Ux \rangle = \|x\|^2 = \langle x, x \rangle$ for all $x$. Polarise:
    $\langle Ux, Uy \rangle = \tfrac{1}{4}(\|U(x+y)\|^2 - \|U(x-y)\|^2 - i\|U(x+iy)\|^2 + i\|U(x-iy)\|^2) = \tfrac{1}{4}(\|x+y\|^2 - \|x-y\|^2 - i\|x+iy\|^2 + i\|x-iy\|^2) = \langle x, y \rangle$.
    (2 $\Rightarrow$ 3): $\langle x, y \rangle = \langle Ux, Uy \rangle = \langle x, U^* U y \rangle$ for all $x, y$, so
    $U^* U = \mathrm{Id}_V$.
    (3 $\Rightarrow$ 1): $\|Ux\|^2 = \langle Ux, Ux \rangle = \langle x, U^* U x \rangle = \langle x, x \rangle = \|x\|^2$.
    The last remark: (3) gives injectivity of $U$; surjectivity adds
    $U U^* = \mathrm{Id}_W$ by the symmetric argument.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Singular value decomposition)"
    <a id="thm-sa-5-3"></a>
    <a id="thm-singular-value-decomposition"></a>

    For finite-dimensional inner product spaces $V,W$, every linear mapping has a matrix factorization $T=U\Sigma R^*$ in orthonormal coordinates, with $U,R$ unitary and $\Sigma$ rectangular diagonal with nonnegative singular values. Its norm is the largest singular value (zero for the zero map), and the eigenvalues of $T^*T$ are the squared singular values, including the zeros required by the source dimension.

??? proof "Proof"
    In finite dimension diagonalize the positive self-adjoint $T^*T$ in an orthonormal basis $e_i$, with eigenvalues $\sigma_i^2$. For $\sigma_i>0$, the vectors $f_i=Te_i/\sigma_i$ are orthonormal, since $\langle Te_i,Te_j\rangle=\langle e_i,T^*Te_j\rangle$. Complete them to an orthonormal target basis; vectors with $\sigma_i=0$ belong to the kernel. In these source and target bases the matrix of $T$ is rectangular diagonal, proving the factorization and its norm formula.

    For a compact operator between Hilbert spaces the analogous expansion uses the nonzero eigenvalues of the compact positive operator $T^*T$, and compactness is characterized by singular values tending to zero. Square summability characterizes the stronger Hilbert–Schmidt property. These statements are supplemental to the finite-dimensional construction, and the two criteria must not be identified.

!!! example "Example (Polar decomposition)"
    <a id="ex-polar-decomposition"></a>

    For a bounded operator on Hilbert spaces, the polar decomposition is $T=UP$, where $P=(T^*T)^{1/2}$ is positive and $U$ is a partial isometry, zero on $\ker T$, with initial space $\overline{\operatorname{Im}P}$ and final space $\overline{\operatorname{Im}T}$. This kernel condition makes $U$ unique. In finite dimension the square root is constructed by diagonalizing $T^*T$. If $T$ is invertible between the same Hilbert space, $U$ is unitary. A general partial isometry need not extend to a unitary on the whole space.

!!! definition "Definition (Orthogonal and unitary groups)"
    <a id="def-orthogonal-and-unitary-groups"></a>

    Surjective linear isometries form a group under composition, since their inverses are isometries. In the real case it is the orthogonal group $O(V)$; in the complex case it is the unitary group $U(V)$. In finite dimension injectivity of an isometry already gives surjectivity. In infinite dimension it need not: the unilateral shift on a countable orthonormal basis preserves norm but misses the first basis vector.

!!! example "Example (Orthogonal symmetry)"
    <a id="orthogonal-symmetry"></a>

    For a closed subspace $W$ of a Hilbert space, $s_W=2p_W-I$ fixes $W$ and negates $W^\perp$. Since $p_W^2=p_W$, $s_W^2=I$; since $p_W^*=p_W$, $s_W^*=s_W$. Hence $s_W^*s_W=I$, making it a self-adjoint isometric automorphism.
