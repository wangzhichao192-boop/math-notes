# Multilinear Algebra

This chapter builds the three fundamental graded algebras attached to a module
$V$ over a commutative ring $K$: the **tensor algebra** $T(V)$, the
**symmetric algebra** $S(V)$ and the **exterior algebra** $\Lambda(V)$. The
exterior algebra yields the theory of **determinants** of endomorphisms of a
free module and a clean criterion for linear independence in terms of wedge
products.

## Tensor Product

!!! theorem "Theorem (Tensor product via representability)"
    <a id="thm-ml-1-1"></a>
    <a id="thm-tensor-product-via-representability"></a>

    Let $K$ be a commutative unital ring, $V_1, \ldots, V_n$ be $K$-modules. The
    functor $W \mapsto \mathrm{Hom}_K^{(n)}(V_1, \ldots, V_n; W)$ from
    $\mathrm{Mod}_K$ to $\mathrm{Set}$ is representable. The representing
    object is the **tensor product**

    $$
    V_1 \otimes_K V_2 \otimes_K \cdots \otimes_K V_n
    = K^{\oplus (V_1 \times \cdots \times V_n)} / M,
    $$

    where $M$ is the sub-module generated by all
    $e_{(x_1, \ldots, ax_i' + bx_i'', \ldots, x_n)} - a\, e_{(\ldots, x_i', \ldots)} - b\, e_{(\ldots, x_i'', \ldots)}$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Let $(e_x)_{x \in V_1 \times \cdots \times V_n}$ be the canonical
    basis of $F = K^{\oplus (V_1 \times \cdots \times V_n)}$. For any
    $K$-module $W$ and any function $s : V_1 \times \cdots \times V_n \to W$,
    define $f_s : F \to W$ by $f_s(e_x) = s(x)$. The assignment
    $s \mapsto f_s$ is a bijection from $W^{V_1 \times \cdots \times V_n}$
    to $\mathrm{Hom}_K(F, W)$, functorial in $W$.
    For $s$ to be $n$-linear, $f_s$ must vanish on the sub-module $M$
    generated by the relations in the statement. Conversely, if $f_s$
    vanishes on $M$, then $f_s$ descends to a $K$-linear map
    $\bar f_s : F/M \to W$, and $s$ is $n$-linear iff $\bar f_s(e_x) = s(x)$ forces the linearity conditions on $s$. So
    $W \mapsto \mathrm{Hom}_K^{(n)}(V_1, \ldots, V_n; W)$ is in natural
    bijection with $W \mapsto \mathrm{Hom}_K(F/M, W)$, i.e. the tensor
    product $F/M$ represents the $n$-linearity functor.

!!! definition "Definition (Pure tensor)"
    <a id="def-ml-1-2"></a>
    <a id="def-pure-tensor"></a>

    For $(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in V_1 \times \cdots \times V_n$, the class
    of the basis vector $e_{(x_1, \ldots, x_n)}$ in $V_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_n$ is the **pure tensor** $x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n$.
    The universal property: every $n$-linear
    $f : V_1 \times \cdots \times V_n \to W$ factors uniquely through
    $V_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_n$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Associativity)"
    <a id="prop-ml-1-3"></a>
    <a id="prop-associativity"></a>

    For any partition of $\{1, \ldots, n\}$ into consecutive blocks,
    the iterated tensor product is canonically isomorphic to the full
    tensor product via the map
    $x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n \mapsto x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes (x_i \otimes \cdots \otimes x_{i+j}) \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Define the map
    $\iota : V_1 \times \cdots \times V_n \to V_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_{i-1} \otimes (V_i \otimes \cdots \otimes V_{i+j}) \otimes V_{i+j+1} \otimes \cdots \otimes V_n$ by sending $(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ to
    $x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes (x_i \otimes \cdots \otimes x_{i+j}) \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n$. This map is $n$-linear (it is $K$-linear in
    each slot separately), so by the universal property of the tensor
    product it induces a $K$-linear map
    $\bar\iota : V_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_n \to V_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes (V_i \otimes \cdots \otimes V_{i+j}) \otimes \cdots \otimes V_n$.

    To show $\bar\iota$ is an isomorphism, observe that both
    $V_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_n$ and the iterated product represent
    the same functor $W \mapsto \mathrm{Hom}_K^{(n)}(V_1, \ldots, V_n; W)$ (an $n$-linear map factors uniquely through either tensor
    product). Indeed, the iterated $n$-linear hom-space
    $\mathrm{Hom}_K^{(n-j)}(V_1, \ldots, V_{i-1}, (V_i \otimes \cdots \otimes V_{i+j}), V_{i+j+1}, \ldots, V_n; W)$ is the same as
    $\mathrm{Hom}_K^{(n)}(V_1, \ldots, V_n; W)$ by the universal property
    of the inner tensor product. By the Yoneda lemma ([Theorem](category-theory.md#thm-yoneda)),
    both tensor products are isomorphic to the representing object, so
    they are isomorphic to each other.

!!! notation "Empty tensor products and multilinearity"
    <a id="notation-empty-tensor-products-and-multilinearity"></a>

    The zero-fold tensor product is $K$. A zero-linear map into $W$ is the choice of an element of $W$, represented by the map $K\to W$ sending $1$ to that element. All algebras and algebra homomorphisms here preserve the unit.

## Tensor Algebra

!!! definition "Definition (Tensor algebra)"
    <a id="def-ml-2-1"></a>
    <a id="def-tensor-algebra"></a>

    Let $V$ be a $K$-module and $V^{\otimes n} = V \otimes_K \cdots \otimes_K V$ ($n$ copies, $V^{\otimes 0} = K$). The **tensor
    algebra** of $V$ is the $K$-algebra

    $$
    T(V) = \bigoplus_{n \in \mathbb{N}} V^{\otimes n},
    $$

    with multiplication induced by the concatenation of pure tensors:
    $(x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n) \cdot (y_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes y_m) = x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n \otimes y_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes y_m$. It is a **graded $K$-algebra**.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Universal property of $T(V)$)"
    <a id="thm-ml-2-2"></a>
    <a id="thm-universal-property-of"></a>

    The functor $A \mapsto \mathrm{Hom}_K(V, A)$ from $\mathrm{Alg}_K$ to
    $\mathrm{Set}$ is represented by $T(V)$. In particular, every
    $K$-linear map $f : V \to A$ extends uniquely to a $K$-algebra
    homomorphism $\lambda_f : T(V) \to A$ with
    $\lambda_f(x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n) = f(x_1) \cdots f(x_n)$.

??? proof "Proof"
    **Uniqueness.** $T(V)$ is generated as a $K$-algebra by $V$ (embedded
    via $v \mapsto v$). So any $K$-algebra map $\lambda_f : T(V) \to A$
    extending $f$ is determined by
    $\lambda_f(v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_n) = \lambda_f(v_1) \cdots \lambda_f(v_n) = f(v_1) \cdots f(v_n)$.
    **Existence.** Define the $n$-linear map
    $V^n \to A$, $(v_1, \ldots, v_n) \mapsto f(v_1) \cdots f(v_n)$.
    By the universal property of the tensor product this induces a
    $K$-linear map $\lambda_f^{(n)} : V^{\otimes n} \to A$ with
    $\lambda_f^{(n)}(v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_n) = f(v_1) \cdots f(v_n)$.
    In degree zero set $\lambda_f^{(0)}(a)=a1_A$; this ensures that the map preserves the unit.
    The family $(\lambda_f^{(n)})_n$ assembles into a $K$-linear map
    $\lambda_f : T(V) = \bigoplus V^{\otimes n} \to A$, which is
    multiplicative by
    $\lambda_f(u \otimes v) = f(u_1) \cdots f(u_p) f(v_1) \cdots f(v_q) = \lambda_f(u) \lambda_f(v)$ for pure tensors $u \in V^{\otimes p}$,
    $v \in V^{\otimes q}$, and extends by linearity.

!!! corollary "Corollary (Functoriality of $T$)"
    <a id="cor-ml-2-3"></a>
    <a id="cor-functoriality-of"></a>

    A $K$-linear map $f : V \to W$ induces a $K$-algebra homomorphism
    $T(f) : T(V) \to T(W)$ with
    $T(f)(x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n) = f(x_1) \otimes \cdots \otimes f(x_n)$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Apply [Theorem](#thm-universal-property-of) with the target algebra $A = T(W)$ and the
    $K$-linear map $V \to T(W)$, $v \mapsto f(v)$ (well defined since
    $f$ is $K$-linear). The image of $x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n$
    is $f(x_1) \cdots f(x_n) = f(x_1) \otimes \cdots \otimes f(x_n)$ in
    $T(W)$, so the resulting $K$-algebra map $T(V) \to T(W)$ has the
    stated form.

!!! definition "Definition (Graded algebra, homogeneous ideal)"
    <a id="def-ml-2-4"></a>
    <a id="def-graded-algebra-homogeneous-ideal"></a>

    A **graded $K$-algebra** is a $K$-algebra $A = \bigoplus_{n \geq 0} A_n$ with $A_n \cdot A_m \subseteq A_{n+m}$. An ideal $I \subseteq A$
    is **homogeneous** if it is generated by homogeneous elements; then
    $I = \bigoplus_{n \geq 0} I \cap A_n$ and $A / I$ inherits a
    grading.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Homogeneous components and quotients)"
    <a id="homogeneous-components-quotients"></a>

    Every element of a graded algebra has a unique finite sum of homogeneous components. An ideal $I$ is homogeneous if and only if every homogeneous component of every element of $I$ belongs to $I$. In that case $(A/I)_n=A_n/(I\cap A_n)$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Uniqueness and finiteness are the direct-sum property. If $I$ is generated by homogeneous elements, expand both multipliers in every product $agb$, with $g$ a homogeneous generator. Each homogeneous component of such a product is a sum of products of homogeneous factors and belongs to $I$. Conversely, if components belong to $I$, the homogeneous elements of $I$ generate it. The degree components in the quotient are independent because a sum lying in $I$ has each component in $I$, and products respect the sum of degrees.

## Symmetric Algebra

!!! definition "Definition (Construction of the symmetric algebra)"
    <a id="symmetric-algebra-construction"></a>

    Let $I$ be the two-sided ideal of $T(V)$ generated by $x\otimes y-y\otimes x$, for $x,y\in V$. Define $S(V)=T(V)/I$. The generators have degree two, so this quotient is graded.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Universal property of the symmetric algebra)"
    <a id="symmetric-algebra-universal-property"></a>

    For every commutative unital $K$-algebra $A$, restriction to $V$ gives a natural bijection between unital $K$-algebra maps $S(V)\to A$ and $K$-linear maps $V\to A$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Since every permutation is a product of transpositions
    ([Proposition](differential-calculus.md#permutation-cycle-decomposition)), and
    $x \otimes y - y \otimes x \in I$ generates a homogeneous ideal
    stable under adjacent transpositions, the relation
    $x_{\sigma(1)} \otimes \cdots \otimes x_{\sigma(n)} - x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n \in I$ holds for every $\sigma \in S_n$. Hence $S(V)$ is
    commutative.
    For any commutative $A$ and $K$-linear $f : V \to A$, the
    $K$-algebra map $\lambda_f : T(V) \to A$ ([Theorem](#thm-universal-property-of))
    satisfies $\lambda_f(x \otimes y - y \otimes x) = f(x) f(y) - f(y) f(x) = 0$ by commutativity of $A$. So $\lambda_f$ vanishes on $I$
    and descends to a $K$-algebra map $S(V) \to A$ extending $f$.
    Uniqueness: $S(V)$ is generated as a $K$-algebra by the image of $V$,
    so any such map is determined by its restriction to $V$.

!!! definition "Definition (Symmetric algebra, polynomial algebra)"
    <a id="def-ml-3-2"></a>
    <a id="def-symmetric-algebra-polynomial-algebra"></a>

    The **symmetric algebra** $S(V) = \bigoplus_{n \geq 0} S^n(V)$ is a
    graded commutative $K$-algebra with $S^0(V) = K$ and $S^1(V) = V$.
    For $V$ a free module with basis $(T_1, \ldots, T_d)$, $S(V) = K[T_1, \ldots, T_d]$ is the **polynomial algebra** in $d$ variables.

??? proof "Proof"
    For a free module on $(T_i)_{i\in I}$, a linear map $V\to A$ is precisely a choice of elements $a_i\in A$. The universal property therefore identifies $S(V)$ with the polynomial algebra $K[T_i:i\in I]$, whose polynomials use finitely many variables. The two universal maps send each $T_i$ to itself; their composites fix the algebra generators, so they are inverse.

## Exterior Algebra

!!! definition "Definition (Exterior algebra, wedge product)"
    <a id="def-ml-4-1"></a>
    <a id="def-exterior-algebra-wedge-product"></a>

    The **exterior algebra** of a $K$-module $V$ is the quotient
    $K$-algebra

    $$
    \Lambda(V) = T(V) / J,\qquad
    J = \langle\, x \otimes x : x \in V \,\rangle.
    $$

    Its homogeneous component of degree $n$ is $\Lambda^n(V)$, and the
    product of $\Lambda(V)$ is the **wedge product** $\wedge$. For
    $(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in V^n$, $x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n$ is the
    class of $x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n$.

!!! definition "Definition (Sign of a permutation)"
    <a id="def-ml-4-2"></a>
    <a id="def-sign-of-a-permutation"></a>

    The **sign homomorphism** $\mathrm{sgn} : S_n \to \{\pm 1\}$ sends
    every transposition to $-1$. Explicitly,

    $$
    \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = \prod_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} \frac{\sigma(j) -
    \sigma(i)}{j - i}.
    $$

    The sign is multiplicative, well-defined (every $\sigma$ is a
    product of transpositions, with sign $(-1)^{\text{number}}$), and
    depends only on the cycle structure of $\sigma$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Anticommutativity)"
    <a id="prop-ml-4-3"></a>
    <a id="prop-anticommutativity"></a>

    For any $(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in V^n$ and $\sigma \in S_n$,

    $$
    x_{\sigma(1)} \wedge \cdots \wedge x_{\sigma(n)} = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\,
    x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n.
    $$

    In particular $x \wedge y = - y \wedge x$ and $x \wedge x = 0$.

??? proof "Proof"
    For $n = 2$, expand $(x + y) \wedge (x + y) = x \wedge x + x \wedge y + y \wedge x + y \wedge y = x \wedge y + y \wedge x$
    (using $x \wedge x = 0$ and $y \wedge y = 0$), so
    $0 = (x + y) \wedge (x + y) = x \wedge y + y \wedge x$, i.e.
    $y \wedge x = -x \wedge y$.
    For general $\sigma$, by [Proposition](differential-calculus.md#permutation-cycle-decomposition) every $\sigma \in S_n$ is a
    product of transpositions $(i\; j)$. By the $n = 2$ case,
    each transposition contributes a factor $-1$ (the remaining
    variables are unchanged), so the result is $\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \cdot x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n$. Explicitly, it suffices to
    check on adjacent transpositions $(i\; i+1)$:
    $x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_{i+1} \wedge x_i \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n = -x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n$ (swap the two
    consecutive slots and use $x \wedge y = -y \wedge x$).

!!! proposition "Proposition (Universal property of $\Lambda^n$)"
    <a id="prop-ml-4-4"></a>
    <a id="prop-universal-property-of"></a>

    Every alternating $n$-linear $f : V^n \to M$ factors uniquely
    through a $K$-linear $\hat f : \Lambda^n(V) \to M$ with
    $\hat f(x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n) = f(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$.

??? proof "Proof"
    **Uniqueness.** The pure tensors $x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n$ span
    $\Lambda^n(V)$ (by definition), so any $K$-linear $\hat f$ on
    $\Lambda^n(V)$ is determined by its values on pure tensors, which
    are forced by the relation.
    **Existence.** By the universal property of the tensor product
    ([Theorem](#thm-tensor-product-via-representability)), $f$ induces a $K$-linear map $\tilde f : V^{\otimes n} \to M$ with $\tilde f(x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n) = f(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$. Since $f$ is alternating, $\tilde f$ vanishes on every
    element of the form $x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n$ with two
    equal slots (and hence on the sub-module generated by such
    elements, which is precisely the kernel of $V^{\otimes n} \to \Lambda^n(V)$). So $\tilde f$ descends to a $K$-linear map
    $\hat f : \Lambda^n(V) \to M$ with $\hat f(x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n) = f(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$.

    **Consequence.** If $f(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \neq 0$ for some
    alternating $n$-linear $f$, then $\hat f(x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n) = f(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \neq 0$, forcing $x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n \neq 0$.

!!! example "Example (Wedge of linear forms)"
    <a id="ex-ml-4-5"></a>
    <a id="ex-wedge-of-linear-forms"></a>

    Given $n$ linear forms $f_1, \ldots, f_n$ on $V$, the formula

    $$
    (f_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge f_n)(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n}
    \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\, f_{\sigma(1)}(x_1) \cdots f_{\sigma(n)}(x_n)
    $$

    gives an alternating $n$-linear form, hence a linear form
    $f_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge f_n \in \Lambda^n(V)^\vee$. With $(e_i)$
    a basis of $V$ and $(e_i^\vee)$ its dual,
    $(e_1^\vee \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n^\vee)(e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n) = 1$.

??? proof "Proof"
    For the sign formula, write $D(t_1,\ldots,t_n)=\prod_{i<j}(t_j-t_i)$ over the integers. Swapping adjacent variables reverses exactly one factor and pairs all other affected factors, so changes $D$ to $-D$. Every permutation is a product of adjacent transpositions; hence $D(t_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,t_{\sigma(n)})$ is $\pm D(t_1,\ldots,t_n)$. Its ratio evaluated at $(1,\ldots,n)$ is the displayed sign. Substitution of two successive permutations proves multiplicativity; in particular this proves that the parity of a decomposition is independent of that decomposition. The displayed quotients are computed over the rationals to obtain the integer $\pm1$, then mapped into $K$; they do not require division in $K$. A cycle of length $r$ has sign $(-1)^{r-1}$, since it is a product of $r-1$ transpositions.

!!! definition "Definition (Alternating multilinear map)"
    <a id="alternating-multilinear-map"></a>

    A multilinear map is alternating when its value is zero whenever two adjacent arguments coincide. Expanding its value with those arguments both equal to $x+y$ shows that interchanging adjacent arguments changes the sign. Moving any two equal arguments next to each other consequently shows that the value vanishes whenever any two arguments coincide. This definition works in characteristic two as well: antisymmetry alone would not suffice there.

??? proof "Proof"
    In the degree-$n$ part of the ideal generated by $x\otimes x$, the generators are tensors with two equal adjacent arguments, surrounded by arbitrary tensors. Multilinear expansion shows that they span that part of the ideal. This describes the kernel used in the exterior-power universal property directly. For the wedge of linear forms, multilinearity follows term by term; if two adjacent inputs coincide, pairing each permutation with the permutation interchanging the corresponding positions produces equal products with opposite signs. Thus their sum vanishes even in characteristic two. Evaluation on distinct dual basis vectors leaves just the identity permutation, giving value one.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Functoriality of the exterior algebra)"
    <a id="exterior-algebra-functoriality"></a>

    A linear map $f:V\to W$ induces a graded algebra map $\Lambda(f):\Lambda(V)\to\Lambda(W)$ with

    $$
    \Lambda^n(f)(x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_n)=f(x_1)\wedge\cdots\wedge f(x_n).
    $$

    Identity maps and composition are preserved.

??? proof "Proof"
    The tensor-algebra map induced by $f$ sends each generator $x\otimes x$ of the exterior ideal to $f(x)\otimes f(x)$, so descends to the quotient. Its value on each pure wedge is the displayed formula, and these wedges span every degree. The formulas therefore verify the identity and composition laws, as well as uniqueness. In particular, applying this construction to an isomorphism and its inverse gives mutually inverse exterior-algebra maps.

## Linear Independence Criterion

!!! lemma "Lemma (Wedge and linear combinations)"
    <a id="lem-ml-5-1"></a>
    <a id="lem-wedge-and-linear-combinations"></a>

    If $x_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{i, j} e_j$ in a $K$-module $V$, then

    $$
    x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n = \left( \sum_{\sigma \in S_n}
    \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\, a_{1, \sigma(1)} \cdots a_{n, \sigma(n)} \right)
    e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n.
    $$

??? proof "Proof"
    By multilinearity of the wedge, expand
    $x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n = \sum_{j_1, \ldots, j_n} a_{1, j_1} \cdots a_{n, j_n} e_{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{j_n}$.
    The terms with two equal indices (some $j_i = j_k$) vanish
    because $e_j \wedge e_j = 0$. So only $n$-tuples with all $j_i$
    distinct contribute. For a permutation $\sigma$, the term
    $e_{j_{\sigma(1)}} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{j_{\sigma(n)}}$ is
    equal to $\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) e_{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{j_n}$ by [Proposition](#prop-anticommutativity). Set $j_i = \sigma(i)$: the sum over
    distinct $n$-tuples becomes $\sum_\sigma \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) a_{1, \sigma(1)} \cdots a_{n, \sigma(n)}$ times $e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Basis of $\Lambda^d(V)$ from a basis of $V$)"
    <a id="prop-ml-5-2"></a>
    <a id="prop-basis-of-from-a-basis-of"></a>

    Let $(e_1, \ldots, e_n)$ be a basis of a free $K$-module $V$ and
    $d \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$. The family

    $$
    \{e_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_d} : 1 \leq i_1 < \cdots < i_d
    \leq n\}
    $$

    is a basis of $\Lambda^d(V)$ over $K$. In particular
    $\operatorname{rank}_K \Lambda^d(V) = \binom{n}{d}$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Expanding each factor in the basis shows that pure wedges are sums of wedges of basis vectors. Repeated indices give zero, and sorting the remaining indices changes only a sign. This proves spanning. For an increasing $d$-tuple $I$, apply the alternating form $e_{i_1}^\vee\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_d}^\vee$ to a relation between these wedges. Its value is one on the wedge indexed by $I$ and zero on all the others, so the corresponding coefficient is zero. Every coefficient therefore vanishes. This also proves the assertions for $d=0$ and $d>n$: the respective modules are $K$ and zero.

!!! corollary "Corollary (Adjunction formula)"
    <a id="cor-ml-5-3"></a>
    <a id="cor-adjunction-formula"></a>

    For a free module $V$ of rank $n$ and $d \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$, every
    $K$-linear map $F : \Lambda^{n-d}(V) \to \Lambda^n(V)$ is of the form
    $F(\beta) = \alpha \wedge \beta$ for a unique $\alpha \in \Lambda^d(V)$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Fix a basis and put $\eta=e_1\wedge\cdots\wedge e_n$. For each increasing $d$-subset $I$, let $I^c$ be its increasing complement, write $e_I$ for its wedge, and let $\epsilon_I\in\{1,-1\}$ satisfy $e_I\wedge e_{I^c}=\epsilon_I\eta$. Write $F(e_{I^c})=c_I\eta$ and set

    $$
    \alpha=\sum_{|I|=d}\epsilon_I c_I e_I.
    $$

    In $\alpha\wedge e_{J^c}$ every summand except $I=J$ has a repeated basis vector, and the remaining summand equals $c_J\eta$. Thus the proposed map agrees with $F$ on a basis and hence everywhere. Conversely, wedging with $e_{I^c}$ recovers each coefficient of $\alpha$, which proves uniqueness. The determinant line $\Lambda^n(V)$ is essential to this identification; no basis-free identification of $\Lambda^{n-d}(V)^\vee$ with $\Lambda^d(V)$ alone is being assumed.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Wedge criterion and generators)"
    <a id="wedge-independence-criterion"></a>

    If $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ belong to a module over a commutative ring and $\lambda(x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_n)\ne0$ for every nonzero $\lambda\in K$, then the family is linearly independent. If $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$ generates $V$, the increasing $d$-fold wedges of these generators generate $\Lambda^d(V)$; in particular $\Lambda^d(V)=0$ for $d>n$. For a free rank-$n$ module they form a basis, including $\Lambda^0(V)=K$.

??? proof "Proof"
    In a relation $\sum_i\lambda_i x_i=0$, wedge with all factors except $x_k$. The repeated-factor terms vanish and the remaining term is $\pm\lambda_k x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_n$, so each $\lambda_k=0$. For the generation assertion, expand each argument in the generators, discard repeated indices, and sort the rest. The basis assertion is proved above by evaluation on wedges of dual basis vectors. Over a field the annihilator condition is simply that the wedge is nonzero; over a general ring nonzero alone is insufficient.

## Determinant

!!! definition "Definition (Determinant of an endomorphism)"
    <a id="def-ml-6-1"></a>
    <a id="def-determinant-of-an-endomorphism"></a>

    Let $V$ be a free $K$-module of rank $n$ and $\varphi : V \to V$ a
    $K$-linear endomorphism. The **determinant** of $\varphi$ is the
    unique element $\det(\varphi) \in K$ such that

    $$
    \varphi(x_1) \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi(x_n) = \det(\varphi)\,
    x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n
    $$

    for all $(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in V^n$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Multiplicativity)"
    <a id="prop-ml-6-2"></a>
    <a id="prop-multiplicativity"></a>

    For $\varphi, \psi \in \mathrm{End}_K(V)$,
    $\det(\psi \circ \varphi) = \det(\psi) \det(\varphi)$ and
    $\det(\mathrm{Id}_V) = 1$.

??? proof "Proof"
    The induced map on the free rank-one module $\Lambda^n(V)$ is multiplication by $\det\varphi$. Composition of induced maps multiplies these scalars, and the identity induces the identity. This proves multiplicativity and $\det(\mathrm{Id})=1$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Invertibility and determinant)"
    <a id="determinant-invertibility"></a>

    An endomorphism of a finite free module is invertible if and only if its determinant is a unit of $K$.

??? proof "Proof"
    If $\varphi$ has an inverse, multiplicativity gives $\det\varphi\det(\varphi^{-1})=1$. Conversely, suppose $\delta=\det\varphi$ is a unit, choose a basis $(e_i)$, and put $x_i=\varphi(e_i)$. Since $x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_n=\delta\eta$, this wedge has zero annihilator; the wedge criterion below shows that $(x_i)$ is independent.

    Let $g=\delta^{-1}(e_1^\vee\wedge\cdots\wedge e_n^\vee)$, so $g(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=1$. Define an $(n+1)$-linear map with values in $V$ by

    $$
    F(y_0,\ldots,y_n)=\sum_{i=0}^n(-1)^i g(y_0,\ldots,\widehat{y_i},\ldots,y_n)y_i.
    $$

    If adjacent arguments coincide, the terms omitting neither vanish and the other two cancel. Thus $F$ is alternating and factors through $\Lambda^{n+1}(V)=0$. Evaluating $F(y,x_1,\ldots,x_n)=0$ yields

    $$
    y=\sum_{i=1}^n(-1)^{i+1}g(y,x_1,\ldots,\widehat{x_i},\ldots,x_n)x_i.
    $$

    Consequently the independent family $(x_i)$ also generates $V$. Mapping $x_i$ back to $e_i$ defines the inverse of $\varphi$. The rank-zero case is immediate.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Leibniz formula)"
    <a id="prop-ml-6-4"></a>
    <a id="prop-leibniz-formula"></a>

    For the matrix $A = (a_{i, j}) \in M_n(K)$,

    $$
    \det(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\, a_{1, \sigma(1)}
    \cdots a_{n, \sigma(n)}.
    $$

??? proof "Proof"
    Let $(e_1, \ldots, e_n)$ be the canonical basis of $K^n$. For
    each $i$, the image of $e_i$ is $\sum_j a_{i,j}e_j$ in the course's row-vector convention. By
    [Lemma](#lem-wedge-and-linear-combinations),
    $A e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge A e_n = \left( \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) a_{1, \sigma(1)} \cdots a_{n, \sigma(n)} \right) e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n$.
    By definition of the determinant,
    $A e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge A e_n = \det(A)\, e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n$. Since $\{e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n\}$ is a basis of
    $\Lambda^n(K^n) \cong K$ ([Proposition](#prop-basis-of-from-a-basis-of)), the coefficient must
    equal $\det(A)$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Determinant of the dual endomorphism)"
    <a id="prop-ml-6-5"></a>
    <a id="prop-determinant-of-the-dual-endomorphism"></a>

    For a free $K$-module $V$ of finite rank, $\det(\varphi^\vee) = \det(\varphi)$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Let $(e_i)$ be a basis of $V$ and $(e_i^\vee)$ its dual basis. Then
    $(e_1^\vee \circ \varphi) \wedge \cdots \wedge (e_n^\vee \circ \varphi)$ is an element of $\Lambda^n(V^\vee)$, which is
    one-dimensional. Evaluating on $e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n$ gives
    $(e_1^\vee \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n^\vee)(\varphi(e_1) \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi(e_n)) = \det(\varphi)$ (apply each
    $e_i^\vee$ to the corresponding $\varphi(e_j)$ and use the
    determinant form of the wedge). So
    $(e_1^\vee \circ \varphi) \wedge \cdots \wedge (e_n^\vee \circ \varphi) = \det(\varphi)\, e_1^\vee \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n^\vee$,
    which by definition gives $\det(\varphi^\vee) = \det(\varphi)$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Adjugate identities)"
    <a id="prop-ml-6-6"></a>
    <a id="prop-adjugate-identities"></a>

    The **adjugate** $\mathrm{adj}(A)$ of a square matrix $A$ satisfies
    $A\, \mathrm{adj}(A) = \mathrm{adj}(A)\, A = \det(A) \mathrm{Id}$.
    In particular, when $\det A$ is invertible, $A^{-1} = \det(A)^{-1} \mathrm{adj}(A)$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Define $\mathrm{adj}(A)_{k,j}=C_{j,k}$. The $(i, j)$ entry of $A \cdot \mathrm{adj}(A)$
    is $\sum_k a_{i, k} C_{j, k}$ where $C_{j, k} = (-1)^{j+k} M_{j, k}$ is the $(j, k)$-cofactor. By Laplace expansion along
    row $j$, this is exactly the determinant when $i = j$ and $0$
    when $i \neq j$ (the matrix obtained by replacing row $j$ with row
    $i \neq j$ has two equal rows). So
    $(A\, \mathrm{adj}(A))_{i, j} = \delta_{i, j} \det A$.
    The computation for $\mathrm{adj}(A) A$ is analogous. When $\det A$ is a unit, the matrix $\det(A)^{-1}\mathrm{adj}(A)$ is both a left and a right inverse by the two identities, giving the asserted inverse formula.

??? proof "Proof"
    Existence of the determinant follows by applying functoriality of exterior powers to $\varphi$: every endomorphism of the free rank-one module $\Lambda^n(V)$ is multiplication by a unique scalar. The wedge formula for matrices is the expansion in a basis. Replacing a permutation by its inverse in the Leibniz sum proves $\det(A^{\mathsf T})=\det(A)$, also following from the result for the dual endomorphism.

## Adjugate and Laplace Expansion

!!! definition "Definition (Intrinsic adjugate)"
    <a id="intrinsic-adjugate"></a>

    Let $V$ be free of finite positive rank $n$. For $x\in V$, the adjunction formula gives a unique $\varphi^{a}(x)\in V$ such that

    $$
    \varphi^{a}(x)\wedge y_2\wedge\cdots\wedge y_n
    =x\wedge\varphi(y_2)\wedge\cdots\wedge\varphi(y_n).
    $$

    This defines the adjugate endomorphism $\varphi^{a}$, and $\varphi^{a}\varphi=(\det\varphi)\mathrm{Id}_V$.

??? proof "Proof"
    For fixed $x$ the right side is alternating and multilinear in the remaining arguments, so it defines a map $\Lambda^{n-1}(V)\to\Lambda^n(V)$. Adjunction gives existence and uniqueness. Addition and scalar multiplication in $x$, together with uniqueness, prove linearity. For $x=\varphi(z)$ the defining right side equals $(\det\varphi)z\wedge y_2\wedge\cdots\wedge y_n$; uniqueness yields the asserted composition identity.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Cofactors and expansion along a row or column)"
    <a id="laplace-expansion"></a>

    Let $A^{[i,j]}$ be the matrix obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$, and put $C_{i,j}=(-1)^{i+j}\det A^{[i,j]}$. In the course's row-vector convention the matrix of $\varphi^{a}$ has entries $(\varphi^a)_{j,i}=C_{i,j}$. For every row $i$ and column $j$,

    $$
    \det A=\sum_k a_{i,k}C_{i,k}=\sum_k a_{k,j}C_{k,j}.
    $$

??? proof "Proof"
    Expand the defining wedge of $\varphi^a(e_j)$ against all basis vectors except $e_i$. Only its $e_i$ coordinate survives. Moving the omitted basis vector to its position introduces the sign $(-1)^{i+j}$, and the remaining wedge coefficient is the determinant of the deleted minor. This gives the transposed cofactor matrix. Alternatively group the Leibniz sum according to the value of the permutation in row $i$; the residual permutation on the complementary indices has precisely that sign, proving row expansion. Transposing proves column expansion. The adjugate identities proved above now follow by expanding a matrix with one row or column replaced; if the replacement duplicates a different row or column its determinant is zero.
