# Bilinear Algebra

This chapter studies **linear forms, bilinear forms, and quadratic forms** on
modules over a commutative ring $K$. The high points are the **dual module**
$V^\vee$, the **polarisation** that recovers a symmetric bilinear form from a
quadratic form (when $2$ is invertible), the existence of an **orthogonal
basis** for finite-dimensional quadratic forms, and **Sylvester's law of
inertia** that classifies real quadratic forms by the signature
$(\dim V_+, \dim V_-, \dim V_0)$.

## Linear Forms and the Dual

!!! definition "Definition (Dual module)"
    <a id="def-bl-1-1"></a>
    <a id="def-dual-module"></a>

    Let $K$ be a unitary ring and $V$ a left $K$-module. The **dual
    module** is $V^\vee := \mathrm{Hom}_K(V, K)$, equipped with the right
    $K$-module structure $(a, f) \mapsto f(\cdot)\, a$.

    When $V = K^n$ (row vectors) the dual consists of column vectors
    $(a_1, \ldots, a_n)^T$ acting by
    $\langle x, a \rangle = \sum x_i a_i$.

!!! definition "Definition (Dual of a linear map)"
    <a id="def-bl-1-2"></a>
    <a id="def-dual-of-a-linear-map"></a>

    For a homomorphism $\varphi : V \to W$ of left $K$-modules, the
    **dual** $\varphi^\vee : W^\vee \to V^\vee$ is the right-module map
    $g \mapsto g \circ \varphi$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Contravariance of $\vee$)"
    <a id="prop-bl-1-3"></a>
    <a id="prop-contravariance-of"></a>

    For homomorphisms $\varphi : E \to F$ and $\psi : F \to G$ of left
    $K$-modules, $(\psi \circ \varphi)^\vee = \varphi^\vee \circ \psi^\vee$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Dual basis)"
    <a id="prop-bl-1-4"></a>
    <a id="prop-dual-basis"></a>

    If $(e_1, \ldots, e_r)$ is a basis of a free $K$-module $V$ of finite
    rank, then $(e_1^\vee, \ldots, e_r^\vee)$ with
    $e_i^\vee(\lambda_1 e_1 + \cdots + \lambda_r e_r) = \lambda_i$ is a
    basis of $V^\vee$ (the **dual basis**).

??? proof "Proof"
    For any $f \in V^\vee$ and $v = \lambda_1 e_1 + \cdots + \lambda_r e_r \in V$,
    $f(v) = \lambda_1 f(e_1) + \cdots + \lambda_r f(e_r) = \sum_{i=1}^{r} e_i^\vee(v) f(e_i)$. So
    $f = \sum e_i^\vee f(e_i)$ in $V^\vee$. The coefficients $f(e_i)$
    are uniquely determined by $f$ (just apply $f$ to each $e_i$).
    Hence $(e_1^\vee, \ldots, e_r^\vee)$ spans $V^\vee$ and is linearly
    independent (a linear combination $\sum a_i e_i^\vee = 0$ applied
    to $e_j$ gives $a_j = 0$).

??? proof "Proof"
    For contravariance, evaluate at $g\in G^\vee$: $\varphi^\vee(\psi^\vee(g))=(g\circ\psi)\circ\varphi=g\circ(\psi\circ\varphi)$. Right-linearity follows from $(ga)\circ\varphi=(g\circ\varphi)a$.

!!! example "Example (Transpose over a noncommutative ring)"
    <a id="transpose-opposite-ring"></a>

    In the source's row convention, $\varphi:K^n\to K^p$ is $x\mapsto xB$, with $B=(b_{ij})$. A dual column $y$ acts by $v\mapsto\sum_jv_jy_j$. Its composite with $\varphi$ acts by $x\mapsto\sum_i x_i(\sum_jb_{ij}y_j)$, so the dual column is $By$. On viewing right modules as left $K^{\mathrm{op}}$-modules and writing their coordinates as rows, this map is represented by $B^T\in M_{p,n}(K^{\mathrm{op}})$. The opposite ring records the reversed scalar multiplication; it cannot be dropped for a noncommutative $K$.

## Bilinear Forms

!!! definition "Definition (Bilinear form, symmetric, alternating)"
    <a id="def-bl-2-1"></a>
    <a id="def-bilinear-form-symmetric-alternating"></a>

    A **bilinear form** on a $K$-module $V$ ($K$ commutative) is a
    $K$-bilinear map $\varphi : V \times V \to K$. It is
    **symmetric** if $\varphi(x, y) = \varphi(y, x)$ and
    **alternating** if $\varphi(x, x) = 0$ for all $x$.

!!! example "Example (Dot product)"
    <a id="ex-dot-product"></a>

    On $K^n$,
    $\langle (x_1, \ldots, x_n), (y_1, \ldots, y_n) \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i y_i$ is a symmetric bilinear form.

!!! definition "Definition (Orthogonal, orthogonal complement)"
    <a id="def-bl-2-2"></a>
    <a id="def-orthogonal-orthogonal-complement"></a>

    Given a symmetric bilinear form $\varphi$ on $V$, two elements
    $x, y \in V$ are **$\varphi$-orthogonal** if $\varphi(x, y) = 0$
    (written $x \perp_\varphi y$). For $A \subseteq V$, the
    **orthogonal complement** is
    $A^\perp = \{y \in V : \forall x \in A,\ \varphi(x, y) = 0\}$.
    $A^\perp$ is a sub-$K$-module, and $(A^\perp)^\perp$ contains the submodule generated by
    $A$, and $A \subseteq B \Rightarrow B^\perp \subseteq A^\perp$.

!!! definition "Definition (Kernel, non-degeneracy)"
    <a id="def-bl-2-3"></a>
    <a id="def-kernel-non-degeneracy"></a>

    The **kernel** of a symmetric bilinear form $\varphi$ is
    $\ker \ell_\varphi$ where $\ell_\varphi : V \to V^\vee$,
    $x \mapsto \varphi(x, \cdot)$. The form is **non-degenerate** if
    $\ell_\varphi$ is an isomorphism.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Non-degeneracy on finite-dimensional vector spaces)"
    <a id="thm-bl-2-4"></a>
    <a id="thm-non-degeneracy-on-finite-dimensional-vector-spaces"></a>

    Let $K$ be a field and $V$ a finite-dimensional $K$-vector space. A
    symmetric bilinear form $\varphi$ on $V$ is non-degenerate iff its
    kernel is $\{0\}$.

??? proof "Proof"
    If $\ell_\varphi$ is an isomorphism, its kernel is necessarily $\{0\}$.
    Conversely, if $\ker \ell_\varphi = \{0\}$, then $\ell_\varphi$ is
    injective. By rank–nullity,
    $\dim V = \dim \ker \ell_\varphi + \dim \mathrm{Im}\, \ell_\varphi = \dim \mathrm{Im}\, \ell_\varphi$, Since $\dim V^\vee=\dim V$, its image equals $V^\vee$, so $\ell_\varphi$ is surjective.
    Hence $\ell_\varphi$ is bijective, i.e. $\varphi$ is non-degenerate.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Orthogonal-complement identities)"
    <a id="prop-orthogonal-complement-identities"></a>

    The orthogonal complement is a submodule: $\varphi(z,ax+by)=a\varphi(z,x)+b\varphi(z,y)=0$ for $x,y\in A^\perp$ and $z\in A$. Symmetry gives $A\subseteq(A^\perp)^\perp$, and this latter submodule therefore contains $\operatorname{Span}A$. Inclusion reverses because orthogonality to every member of a larger set imposes all conditions from the smaller set.

## Quadratic Forms

!!! definition "Definition (Quadratic form, polarisation)"
    <a id="def-bl-3-1"></a>
    <a id="def-quadratic-form-polarisation"></a>

    A **quadratic form** on a $K$-module $V$ is a map $q : V \to K$ such
    that $q(x) = \varphi(x, x)$ for some bilinear form $\varphi$. When
    $2$ is invertible in $K$, the **polarisation** is the symmetric
    bilinear form

    $$
    \varphi_q(x, y) = \tfrac{1}{2}(q(x + y) - q(x) - q(y)),
    $$

    and the correspondence $q \leftrightarrow \varphi_q$ is a
    $K$-linear isomorphism between quadratic forms and symmetric
    bilinear forms.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Basic properties of quadratic forms)"
    <a id="prop-bl-3-2"></a>
    <a id="prop-basic-properties-of-quadratic-forms"></a>

    For any quadratic form $q$ and $a \in K$, $q(a x) = a^2 q(x)$. The
    **isotropic cone** $C(q) := \{x : q(x) = 0\}$ satisfies
    $a \cdot C(q) \subseteq C(q)$, and $\ker q \subseteq C(q)$ (equality
    when $q$ is semi-definite).

!!! proposition "Proposition (Orthogonal basis for symmetric forms)"
    <a id="prop-bl-3-3"></a>
    <a id="prop-orthogonal-basis-for-symmetric-forms"></a>

    Let $K$ be a field with $2$ invertible, $V$ a finite-dimensional
    $K$-vector space, and $\varphi$ any symmetric bilinear form on $V$.
    There is a basis $(e_i)$ of $V$ that is $\varphi$-orthogonal.

??? proof "Proof"
    Induction on $\dim V$. The case $\dim V = 0$ is trivial.
    If $\varphi = 0$ then any basis is orthogonal. Otherwise there is
    $e_1 \in V$ with $\varphi(e_1, e_1) \neq 0$ because if all diagonal values vanished, polarization and invertibility of two would force the whole symmetric form to vanish. The linear form $\ell_{e_1} : V \to K$,
    $x \mapsto \varphi(e_1, x)$ is non-zero hence surjective (since
    $K$ is a field), so $W := \ker \ell_{e_1}$ has codimension $1$.
    Note $e_1 \notin W$ (since $\ell_{e_1}(e_1) = \varphi(e_1, e_1) \neq 0$). Apply the induction hypothesis to the restriction
    $\varphi|_{W \times W}$: there is a $\varphi$-orthogonal basis
    $(e_2, \ldots, e_n)$ of $W$. By construction, $\ell_{e_1}(e_i) = 0$ for $i \geq 2$, i.e. $\varphi(e_1, e_i) = 0$. So
    $(e_1, \ldots, e_n)$ is a $\varphi$-orthogonal basis of $V$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Canonical form over a field with $1/2 \in K$)"
    <a id="prop-bl-3-4"></a>
    <a id="prop-canonical-form-over-a-field-with"></a>

    Let $K$ be a field with $2$ invertible and $V$ a finite-dimensional
    $K$-vector space. For any quadratic form $q$ on $V$ there is a basis
    $(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_r)$ of $V^\vee$ and $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_r \in K$ with

    $$
    q(x) = \lambda_1\, \alpha_1(x)^2 + \cdots + \lambda_r\, \alpha_r(x)^2.
    $$

??? proof "Proof"
    In coordinates write $q=\sum_{i\leq j}a_{ij}x_ix_j$. If $a_{ii}\neq0$, let $L=\sum_{j<i}a_{ji}x_j+\sum_{j>i}a_{ij}x_j$ and separate $q=a_{ii}x_i^2+Lx_i+\widetilde q$. Then

    $$
    q=a_{ii}\left(x_i+\frac{L}{2a_{ii}}\right)^2+\widetilde q-\frac{L^2}{4a_{ii}}.
    $$

    The remainder has no $x_i$. The new first coordinate has coefficient one on $x_i$, so it is independent of the remaining coordinates; recurse on the remainder.

    If every diagonal coefficient vanishes but $q\neq0$, choose a mixed coefficient $a=a_{ij}\neq0$ and write $q=ax_ix_j+Lx_i+Mx_j+q_1$, where $L,M,q_1$ omit $x_i,x_j$. Set $u=x_i+M/a$ and $v=x_j+L/a$. Then

    $$
    q=auv+q_1-LM/a=\frac a4(u+v)^2-\frac a4(u-v)^2+q_1-LM/a.
    $$

    Since two is invertible, $u+v,u-v$ are independent new coordinates and the remainder omits both. Recurse on the remaining coordinates, retaining any unused zero-square coordinates to obtain a full dual basis. Each step reduces the number of remaining variables, so the algorithm terminates.


!!! proposition "Proposition (Polarization without dividing by two)"
    <a id="quadratic-polarization-ring"></a>

    For $q(x)=\varphi(x,x)$, $q(ax)=a^2q(x)$ and $b_q(x,y)=q(x+y)-q(x)-q(y)=\varphi(x,y)+\varphi(y,x)$ is symmetric bilinear over every commutative ring. If $2$ is invertible, $q(x)=b_q(x,x)/2$, and the polarization $b_q/2$ is the unique symmetric form giving $q$. The kernel of $q$ means the kernel of this polarization. The isotropic cone is stable under scalar multiplication; a form is isotropic when that cone contains a nonzero vector, and anisotropic otherwise.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Independence of an orthogonal family)"
    <a id="prop-independence-of-an-orthogonal-family"></a>

    For an anisotropic quadratic form, every orthogonal family of nonzero vectors is linearly independent. Indeed a finite relation $\sum a_ie_i=0$ paired with $e_j$ gives $a_jq(e_j)=0$, so every coefficient vanishes. This proves independence for arbitrary index sets, since linear relations have finite support.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Diagonal coordinates and their converse)"
    <a id="prop-diagonal-coordinates-and-their-converse"></a>

    For an orthogonal basis $e_i$ with dual basis $\alpha_i$, expansion gives $q(x)=\sum_iq(e_i)\alpha_i(x)^2$, as all mixed terms vanish. Conversely, if such a diagonal expression is given in a dual basis, then $q(e_i+e_j)-q(e_i)-q(e_j)=0$ for $i\neq j$; polarization proves orthogonality of the primal basis.

## Quadratic Forms over $\mathbb{R}$

!!! definition "Definition (Definite / semi-definite forms)"
    <a id="def-bl-4-1"></a>
    <a id="def-definite-semi-definite-forms"></a>

    A quadratic form $q : V \to \mathbb{R}$ on a real vector space $V$ is

    + **positive definite** if $q(x) > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$;
    + **positive semi-definite** if $q(x) \geq 0$ for all $x$;
    + **negative (semi-)definite** if $-q$ is positive (semi-)definite.

    A symmetric bilinear form is **definite** if its associated quadratic
    form is.

!!! lemma "Lemma (Kernel = isotropic cone for semi-definite forms)"
    <a id="lem-bl-4-2"></a>
    <a id="lem-kernel-isotropic-cone-for-semi-definite-forms"></a>

    If $q$ is semi-definite, $\ker q = C(q)$.

??? proof "Proof"
    $C(q) \supseteq \ker q$ is immediate. Conversely, assume $x \in C(q)$.
    For any $y \in V$ and $t \in \mathbb{R}$,
    $0 \leq q(y + t x) = q(y) + 2t \varphi(x, y) + t^2 q(x) = q(y) + 2t \varphi(x, y)$ (since $q(x) = 0$). The same argument with
    $-t$ in place of $t$ gives $0 \leq q(y) - 2t \varphi(x, y)$.
    If $\varphi(x,y)\neq0$, taking $t$ of the opposite sign and sufficiently large magnitude makes $q(y)+2t\varphi(x,y)<0$, a contradiction. Thus $x\in\ker q$. For a negative semidefinite form apply this argument to $-q$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Cauchy–Schwarz, real case)"
    <a id="thm-bl-4-3"></a>
    <a id="thm-cauchyschwarz-real-case"></a>

    Let $\varphi$ be a symmetric bilinear form on a real vector space
    $V$ with positive semi-definite $q(x) := \varphi(x, x)$. Then

    $$
    \varphi(x, y)^2 \leq q(x) q(y),
    $$

    with equality iff the classes of $x, y$ in $V / \ker \varphi$ are
    colinear.

??? proof "Proof"
    If $x \in \ker \varphi$ (equivalently $q(x) = 0$), then
    $\varphi(x, y) = 0$ for every $y$ and the inequality is $0 \leq 0$.
    Otherwise set $t = -\varphi(x, y) / q(x)$. Then
    $0 \leq q(y + t x) = q(y) + 2t \varphi(x, y) + t^2 q(x) = q(y) - 2 \varphi(x, y)^2 / q(x) + \varphi(x, y)^2 / q(x) = q(y) - \varphi(x, y)^2 / q(x)$.
    Rearranging: $\varphi(x, y)^2 \leq q(x) q(y)$.
    Equality holds iff the discriminant is zero, i.e.
    $y + t x \in \ker \varphi$, which means $y$ is colinear with $x$
    in $V / \ker \varphi$.

!!! definition "Definition (Sylvester decomposition, signature)"
    <a id="def-bl-4-4"></a>
    <a id="def-sylvester-decomposition-signature"></a>

    A **Sylvester decomposition** of a quadratic form $q$ on a
    finite-dimensional real vector space $V$ is a pairwise orthogonal direct sum
    $V = V_+ \oplus V_- \oplus V_0$ with
    $q|_{V_+}$ positive definite, $q|_{V_-}$ negative definite, and
    $q|_{V_0} = 0$. The triple
    $(\dim V_+, \dim V_-, \dim V_0)$ is the **Sylvester inertia** (or
    **signature**) of $q$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Sylvester's law of inertia)"
    <a id="thm-bl-4-5"></a>
    <a id="thm-sylvester-s-law-of-inertia"></a>

    Every quadratic form on a finite-dimensional real vector space
    admits a Sylvester decomposition. The dimensions
    $\dim V_+$, $\dim V_-$ and $\dim V_0$ are invariants of the form.

??? proof "Proof"
    **Existence.** Take an orthogonal basis $(e_i)$ of $V$ and its dual basis
    $(\alpha_i)_{i=1}^{r}$, and write
    $q(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \lambda_i \alpha_i(x)^2$. Define
    $V_+ = \bigoplus_{\lambda_i > 0} K e_i$, $V_- = \bigoplus_{\lambda_i < 0} K e_i$, $V_0 = \bigoplus_{\lambda_i = 0} K e_i$ (where
    $(e_i)$ is the basis of $V$ dual to $(\alpha_i)$). Then
    $q|_{V_+}$ is positive definite, $q|_{V_-}$ is negative definite,
    $q|_{V_0} = 0$, and $V = V_+ \oplus V_- \oplus V_0$.
    **Uniqueness.** Suppose $V = V_+ \oplus V_- \oplus V_0$ and
    $V = W_+ \oplus W_- \oplus W_0$ are two Sylvester decompositions.
    Then $q|_{V_- \oplus V_0}$ is negative semi-definite (a sum of
    $\leq 0$ forms) and $q|_{W_+}$ is positive definite. If $x \in (V_- \oplus V_0) \cap W_+$, then $q(x) \leq 0$ and $q(x) \geq 0$,
    so $q(x)=0$. Positive definiteness on $W_+$ forces $x=0$. Hence the intersection is
    $\{0\}$, and
    $\dim W_+ \leq \dim V - \dim(V_- \oplus V_0) = \dim V_+$.
    The symmetric argument with $-q$ gives $\dim W_- \leq \dim V_-$
    and $\dim V_+ \leq \dim W_+$, so $\dim W_+ = \dim V_+$. Hence
    $\dim V_0 = \dim V - \dim V_+ - \dim V_- = \dim W_0$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Direct sum criterion)"
    <a id="prop-bl-4-6"></a>
    <a id="prop-direct-sum-criterion"></a>

    Let $V$ be generated by sub-modules $W_1, W_2$. Then
    $V = W_1 \oplus W_2$ iff $W_1 \cap W_2 = \{0\}$.

??? proof "Proof"
    **$(\Rightarrow)$** If $V = W_1 \oplus W_2$, then any $x \in W_1 \cap W_2$
    can be written as $x = x + 0 = 0 + x$, and uniqueness of the
    decomposition forces $x = 0$.
    **$(\Leftarrow)$** Conversely, the addition map
    $W_1 \oplus W_2 \to V$ is surjective by hypothesis. For injectivity,
    if $x \in W_1$ and $y \in W_2$ with $x + y = 0$, then $x = -y \in W_1 \cap W_2 = \{0\}$, so $x = y = 0$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Modular dimension formula)"
    <a id="prop-bl-4-7"></a>
    <a id="prop-modular-dimension-formula"></a>

    For submodules $E,F$ of a left module $M$ over any unital ring $K$, there is a natural isomorphism
    $E / (E \cap F) \cong (E + F) / F$. In particular, over a field in finite dimension,
    $\dim(E \cap F) + \dim(E + F) = \dim E + \dim F$.

??? proof "Proof"
    The natural map $f : E \to (E + F)/F$ sending $e$ to the class of
    $e$ is well defined and surjective (any element of $E + F$ is
    $e + f$ for some $e \in E$, $f \in F$, and $e + f \equiv e$ mod $F$).
    Its kernel is $\{e \in E : e \in F\} = E \cap F$. By the first
    isomorphism theorem, $E / (E \cap F) \cong (E + F) / F$.
    Apply the rank–nullity theorem to $f$ over the field (or
    use the exact sequence $0 \to E \cap F \to E \oplus F \to E + F \to 0$)
    to obtain the dimension formula.

    **Application.** For a linear mapping $T:V\to W$, the quotient isomorphism $V/\ker T\cong\operatorname{Im}T$ gives rank–nullity by taking dimensions. Its kernel and image belong to different ambient spaces, so the intersection formula is not applied directly to that pair.

!!! definition "Definition (Internal direct sum of an arbitrary family)"
    <a id="def-internal-direct-sum-of-an-arbitrary-family"></a>

    Submodules $V_i$ generate $V$ if the addition map $\bigoplus_iV_i\to V$ is surjective. They form an internal direct sum if that map is an isomorphism: each vector then has a unique expression as a finite sum of vectors from the respective $V_i$. Definiteness and semidefiniteness pass to subspaces by restricting the same inequalities.

!!! proposition "Proposition (The zero component in a Sylvester decomposition)"
    <a id="prop-the-zero-component-in-a-sylvester-decomposition"></a>

    For an orthogonal Sylvester decomposition, $V_0=\ker q$. One inclusion follows because the form vanishes on $V_0$ and its cross terms vanish by orthogonality. If $x=x_++x_-+x_0$ is in the kernel, pairing with $x_+$ and $x_-$ gives $q(x_+)=q(x_-)=0$; definiteness forces both components to vanish.

!!! remark "Remark (Orthogonality is needed in the decomposition)"
    <a id="rem-orthogonality-is-needed-in-the-decomposition"></a>

    The source's printed definition omits the orthogonality condition used by its subsequent proof. Positivity on $V_+$, negativity on $V_-$ and vanishing on $V_0$ alone do not imply the inertia theorem. For example, $q(x,y)=x^2+2xy$ is positive on the first coordinate line and zero on the second, but the second line is not its kernel. The corrected definition includes pairwise orthogonality.
