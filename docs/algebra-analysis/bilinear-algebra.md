# Bilinear Algebra

This chapter studies **linear forms, bilinear forms, and quadratic forms** on
modules over a commutative ring $K$. The high points are the **dual module**
$V^\vee$, the **polarisation** that recovers a symmetric bilinear form from a
quadratic form (when $2$ is invertible), the existence of an **orthogonal
basis** for finite-dimensional quadratic forms, and **Sylvester's law of
inertia** that classifies real quadratic forms by the signature
$(\dim V_+, \dim V_-, \dim V_0)$.

## Linear Forms and the Dual

!!! definition "Definition (Dual module)"
    <a id="def-bl-1-1"></a>
    Let $K$ be a unitary ring and $V$ a left $K$-module. The **dual
    module** is $V^\vee := \mathrm{Hom}_K(V, K)$, equipped with the right
    $K$-module structure $(a, f) \mapsto f(\cdot)\, a$.

    When $V = K^n$ (row vectors) the dual consists of column vectors
    $(a_1, \ldots, a_n)^T$ acting by
    $\langle a, x \rangle = \sum a_i x_i$.

!!! definition "Definition (Dual of a linear map)"
    <a id="def-bl-1-2"></a>
    For a homomorphism $\varphi : V \to W$ of left $K$-modules, the
    **dual** $\varphi^\vee : W^\vee \to V^\vee$ is the right-module map
    $g \mapsto g \circ \varphi$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Contravariance of $\vee$)"
    <a id="prop-bl-1-3"></a>
    For homomorphisms $\varphi : E \to F$ and $\psi : F \to G$ of left
    $K$-modules, $(\psi \circ \varphi)^\vee = \varphi^\vee \circ \psi^\vee$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Dual basis)"
    <a id="prop-bl-1-4"></a>
    If $(e_1, \ldots, e_r)$ is a basis of a free $K$-module $V$ of finite
    rank, then $(e_1^\vee, \ldots, e_r^\vee)$ with
    $e_i^\vee(\lambda_1 e_1 + \cdots + \lambda_r e_r) = \lambda_i$ is a
    basis of $V^\vee$ (the **dual basis**).

??? proof "Proof (Dual basis)"
    For any $f \in V^\vee$ and $v = \lambda_1 e_1 + \cdots + \lambda_r e_r
    \in V$,
    $f(v) = \lambda_1 f(e_1) + \cdots + \lambda_r f(e_r)
    = \sum_{i=1}^{r} f(e_i) \cdot e_i^\vee(v)$. So
    $f = \sum f(e_i) e_i^\vee$ in $V^\vee$. The coefficients $f(e_i)$
    are uniquely determined by $f$ (just apply $f$ to each $e_i$).
    Hence $(e_1^\vee, \ldots, e_r^\vee)$ spans $V^\vee$ and is linearly
    independent (a linear combination $\sum a_i e_i^\vee = 0$ applied
    to $e_j$ gives $a_j = 0$).

## Bilinear Forms

!!! definition "Definition (Bilinear form, symmetric, alternating)"
    <a id="def-bl-2-1"></a>
    A **bilinear form** on a $K$-module $V$ ($K$ commutative) is a
    $K$-bilinear map $\varphi : V \times V \to K$. It is
    **symmetric** if $\varphi(x, y) = \varphi(y, x)$ and
    **alternating** if $\varphi(x, x) = 0$ for all $x$.

!!! example "Example (Dot product)"
    On $K^n$,
    $\langle (x_1, \ldots, x_n), (y_1, \ldots, y_n) \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{n}
    x_i y_i$ is a symmetric bilinear form.

!!! definition "Definition (Orthogonal, orthogonal complement)"
    <a id="def-bl-2-2"></a>
    Given a symmetric bilinear form $\varphi$ on $V$, two elements
    $x, y \in V$ are **$\varphi$-orthogonal** if $\varphi(x, y) = 0$
    (written $x \perp_\varphi y$). For $A \subseteq V$, the
    **orthogonal complement** is
    $A^\perp = \{y \in V : \forall x \in A,\ \varphi(x, y) = 0\}$.
    $A^\perp$ is a sub-$K$-module containing the submodule generated by
    $A$, and $A \subseteq B \Rightarrow B^\perp \subseteq A^\perp$.

!!! definition "Definition (Kernel, non-degeneracy)"
    <a id="def-bl-2-3"></a>
    The **kernel** of a symmetric bilinear form $\varphi$ is
    $\ker \ell_\varphi$ where $\ell_\varphi : V \to V^\vee$,
    $x \mapsto \varphi(x, \cdot)$. The form is **non-degenerate** if
    $\ell_\varphi$ is an isomorphism.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Non-degeneracy on finite-dimensional vector spaces)"
    <a id="thm-bl-2-4"></a>
    Let $K$ be a field and $V$ a finite-dimensional $K$-vector space. A
    symmetric bilinear form $\varphi$ on $V$ is non-degenerate iff its
    kernel is $\{0\}$.

??? proof "Proof (Non-degeneracy)"
    If $\ell_\varphi$ is an isomorphism, its kernel is necessarily $\{0\}$.
    Conversely, if $\ker \ell_\varphi = \{0\}$, then $\ell_\varphi$ is
    injective. By rank–nullity,
    $\dim V = \dim \ker \ell_\varphi + \dim \mathrm{Im}\, \ell_\varphi
    = \dim \mathrm{Im}\, \ell_\varphi$, so $\ell_\varphi$ is surjective.
    Hence $\ell_\varphi$ is bijective, i.e. $\varphi$ is non-degenerate.

## Quadratic Forms

!!! definition "Definition (Quadratic form, polarisation)"
    <a id="def-bl-3-1"></a>
    A **quadratic form** on a $K$-module $V$ is a map $q : V \to K$ such
    that $q(x) = \varphi(x, x)$ for some bilinear form $\varphi$. When
    $2$ is invertible in $K$, the **polarisation** is the symmetric
    bilinear form

    $$
    \varphi_q(x, y) = \tfrac{1}{2}(q(x + y) - q(x) - q(y)),
    $$

    and the correspondence $q \leftrightarrow \varphi_q$ is a
    $K$-linear isomorphism between quadratic forms and symmetric
    bilinear forms.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Basic properties of quadratic forms)"
    <a id="prop-bl-3-2"></a>
    For any quadratic form $q$ and $a \in K$, $q(a x) = a^2 q(x)$. The
    **isotropic cone** $C(q) := \{x : q(x) = 0\}$ satisfies
    $a \cdot C(q) \subseteq C(q)$, and $\ker q \subseteq C(q)$ (equality
    when $q$ is semi-definite).

!!! proposition "Proposition (Orthogonal basis for non-isotropic forms)"
    <a id="prop-bl-3-3"></a>
    Let $K$ be a field with $2$ invertible, $V$ a finite-dimensional
    $K$-vector space, and $\varphi$ a symmetric bilinear form on $V$
    that is **not isotropic** ($\varphi(x, x) \neq 0$ for some $x$).
    There is a basis $(e_i)$ of $V$ that is $\varphi$-orthogonal.

    **Proof (sketch).** Induction on $\dim V$. Pick $e_1$ with
    $\varphi(e_1, e_1) \neq 0$. The linear form
    $\ell_{e_1}(x) = \varphi(e_1, x)$ is non-zero hence surjective, so
    $W = \ker \ell_{e_1}$ has codimension $1$. Apply the induction
    hypothesis to the restriction of $\varphi$ to $W$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Canonical form over a field with $1/2 \in K$)"
    <a id="prop-bl-3-4"></a>
    Let $K$ be a field with $2$ invertible and $V$ a finite-dimensional
    $K$-vector space. For any quadratic form $q$ on $V$ there is a basis
    $(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_r)$ of $V^\vee$ and $\lambda_1, \ldots,
    \lambda_r \in K$ with

    $$
    q(x) = \lambda_1\, \alpha_1(x)^2 + \cdots + \lambda_r\, \alpha_r(x)^2.
    $$

??? proof "Proof (Canonical form via completing the square)"
    Write $q$ in a basis of $V^\vee$ (which gives a basis of $V$ via
    the natural pairing): $q = \sum_{1 \leq i \leq j \leq r} a_{i, j} x_i x_j$.
    **Case 1:** there is $i$ with $a_{i, i} \neq 0$. Split
    $q = a_{i, i} x_i^2 + (2 a_{i, i} x_i) \cdot L(x) + \tilde q(x)$,
    where $L = \sum_{j < i} a_{j, i} x_j + \sum_{j > i} a_{i, j} x_j$ and
    $\tilde q$ contains no $x_i$. Complete the square: with
    $y = x_i + L / a_{i, i}$,
    $q = a_{i, i} y^2 + \tilde q - L^2 / a_{i, i} = a_{i, i} y^2 + q^*$
    where $q^*$ is a quadratic form in $r - 1$ variables (the
    coefficient of $x_i$ in $q^*$ is $0$). Iterate on $q^*$.
    **Case 2:** all $a_{i, i} = 0$. Then there is a cross term
    $a_{i, j} \neq 0$ with $i \neq j$. Split
    $q = a_{i, j} x_i x_j + L_i x_i + L_j x_j + q_1$ where $L_i, L_j$
    are linear forms not involving $x_i, x_j$ and $q_1$ is a quadratic
    form. Set
    $f_1 = a_{i, j}^{-1} x_j + a_{i, j}^{-1} L_i$ and
    $f_2 = a_{i, j}^{-1} x_j - a_{i, j}^{-1} L_j$. Then
    $a_{i, j} x_i x_j + L_i x_i + L_j x_j
    = \tfrac{a_{i, j}}{4}(f_1^2 - f_2^2)$
    (the two independent linear forms $f_1, f_2$). So
    $q = \tfrac{a_{i, j}}{4} f_1^2 - \tfrac{a_{i, j}}{4} f_2^2 + q_2$ for
    a new quadratic form $q_2 = q_1 - a_{i, j} L_i L_j$. The two new
    forms $f_1, f_2$ are linearly independent, and the contribution
    $a_{i, j} f_1 f_2$ has been eliminated. Iterate.
    In both cases, the process terminates because the number of
    "remaining" variables decreases.

## Quadratic Forms over $\mathbb{R}$

!!! definition "Definition (Definite / semi-definite forms)"
    <a id="def-bl-4-1"></a>
    A quadratic form $q : V \to \mathbb{R}$ on a real vector space $V$ is

    + **positive definite** if $q(x) > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$;
    + **positive semi-definite** if $q(x) \geq 0$ for all $x$;
    + **negative (semi-)definite** if $-q$ is positive (semi-)definite.

    A symmetric bilinear form is **definite** if its associated quadratic
    form is.

!!! lemma "Lemma (Kernel = isotropic cone for semi-definite forms)"
    <a id="lem-bl-4-2"></a>
    If $q$ is semi-definite, $\ker q = C(q)$.

??? proof "Proof (Kernel = isotropic cone for semi-definite forms)"
    $C(q) \supseteq \ker q$ is immediate. Conversely, assume $x \in C(q)$.
    For any $y \in V$ and $t \in \mathbb{R}$,
    $0 \leq q(y + t x) = q(y) + 2t \varphi(x, y) + t^2 q(x) = q(y)
    + 2t \varphi(x, y)$ (since $q(x) = 0$). The same argument with
    $-t$ in place of $t$ gives $0 \leq q(y) - 2t \varphi(x, y)$.
    Both must hold for all $t$, so $2t \varphi(x, y) = 0$ in the limit
    $t \to 0^+$ (formally: the quadratic
    $t \mapsto q(y) + 2t \varphi(x, y)$ in $t$ has discriminant
    $\leq 0$, giving $\varphi(x, y) = 0$). So $x \in \ker q$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Cauchy–Schwarz, real case)"
    <a id="thm-bl-4-3"></a>
    Let $\varphi$ be a symmetric bilinear form on a real vector space
    $V$ with positive semi-definite $q(x) := \varphi(x, x)$. Then

    $$
    \varphi(x, y)^2 \leq q(x) q(y),
    $$

    with equality iff the classes of $x, y$ in $V / \ker \varphi$ are
    colinear.

??? proof "Proof (Cauchy–Schwarz, real case)"
    If $x \in \ker \varphi$ (equivalently $q(x) = 0$), then
    $\varphi(x, y) = 0$ for every $y$ and the inequality is $0 \leq 0$.
    Otherwise set $t = -\varphi(x, y) / q(x)$. Then
    $0 \leq q(y + t x) = q(y) + 2t \varphi(x, y) + t^2 q(x)
    = q(y) - 2 \varphi(x, y)^2 / q(x) + \varphi(x, y)^2 / q(x)
    = q(y) - \varphi(x, y)^2 / q(x)$.
    Rearranging: $\varphi(x, y)^2 \leq q(x) q(y)$.
    Equality holds iff the discriminant is zero, i.e.
    $y + t x \in \ker \varphi$, which means $y$ is colinear with $x$
    in $V / \ker \varphi$.

!!! definition "Definition (Sylvester decomposition, signature)"
    <a id="def-bl-4-4"></a>
    A **Sylvester decomposition** of a quadratic form $q$ on a
    finite-dimensional real vector space $V$ is a direct sum
    $V = V_+ \oplus V_- \oplus V_0$ with
    $q|_{V_+}$ positive definite, $q|_{V_-}$ negative definite, and
    $q|_{V_0} = 0$. The triple
    $(\dim V_+, \dim V_-, \dim V_0)$ is the **Sylvester inertia** (or
    **signature**) of $q$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Sylvester's law of inertia)"
    <a id="thm-bl-4-5"></a>
    Every quadratic form on a finite-dimensional real vector space
    admits a Sylvester decomposition. The dimensions
    $\dim V_+$, $\dim V_-$ and $\dim V_0$ are invariants of the form.

    **Proof (existence).** Take an orthogonal basis $(\alpha_i)$ of
    $V^\vee$ and write $q(x) = \sum \lambda_i \alpha_i(x)^2$. Define
    $V_+, V_-, V_0$ by the signs of the $\lambda_i$.

    **Proof (uniqueness).** If $V = V_+ \oplus V_- \oplus V_0$ and
    $V = W_+ \oplus W_- \oplus V_0$ are two Sylvester decompositions,
    then $q|_{V_- \oplus V_0}$ is negative semi-definite and
    $q|_{W_+}$ is positive definite, so
    $(V_- \oplus V_0) \cap W_+ = \{0\}$ and
    $\dim W_+ \leq \dim V - \dim(V_- \oplus V_0) = \dim V_+$. Symmetric
    argument gives the reverse inequality.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Direct sum criterion)"
    <a id="prop-bl-4-6"></a>
    Let $V$ be generated by sub-modules $W_1, W_2$. Then
    $V = W_1 \oplus W_2$ iff $W_1 \cap W_2 = \{0\}$.

??? proof "Proof (Direct sum criterion)"
    **$(\Rightarrow)$** If $V = W_1 \oplus W_2$, then any $x \in W_1 \cap W_2$
    can be written as $x = x + 0 = 0 + x$, and uniqueness of the
    decomposition forces $x = 0$.
    **$(\Leftarrow)$** Conversely, the addition map
    $W_1 \oplus W_2 \to V$ is surjective by hypothesis. For injectivity,
    if $x \in W_1$ and $y \in W_2$ with $x + y = 0$, then $x = -y \in
    W_1 \cap W_2 = \{0\}$, so $x = y = 0$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Modular dimension formula)"
    <a id="prop-bl-4-7"></a>
    For sub-modules $E, F$ of a $K$-module $M$ (where $K$ is a field and
    $M$ is finite-dimensional), there is a natural isomorphism
    $E / (E \cap F) \cong (E + F) / F$. In particular,
    $\dim(E \cap F) + \dim(E + F) = \dim E + \dim F$.

??? proof "Proof (Modular dimension formula)"
    The natural map $f : E \to (E + F)/F$ sending $e$ to the class of
    $e$ is well defined and surjective (any element of $E + F$ is
    $e + f$ for some $e \in E$, $f \in F$, and $e + f \equiv e$ mod $F$).
    Its kernel is $\{e \in E : e \in F\} = E \cap F$. By the first
    isomorphism theorem, $E / (E \cap F) \cong (E + F) / F$.
    Apply the rank–nullity theorem to $f$ in characteristic zero (or
    use the exact sequence $0 \to E \cap F \to E \oplus F \to E + F \to 0$)
    to obtain the dimension formula.

    **Application.** The rank–nullity of a linear map $T : V \to W$
    is recovered by applying the formula to $\ker T$ and $\mathrm{Im}\,
    T$ inside $V$ (for the dimension of $\mathrm{Im}\, T$) and to the
    same sub-modules of $W$ (for $\dim \ker T = \dim V - \dim \mathrm{Im}\,
    T$).
