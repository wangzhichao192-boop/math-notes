# Limit

This chapter fixes the language of **filters** on a set, develops
**order-theoretic limits** in partially ordered groups, and culminates in
the algebraic notion of an **absolute value**, which generalises the
archimedean absolute value on $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}$.

## Filters

!!! definition "Definition (Filter)"
    <a id="def-7-1-1"></a>
    <a id="def-filter"></a>

    A **filter** on a set $X$ is a non-empty subset
    $\mathcal{F} \subseteq \mathscr{P}(X)$ such that

    + $\forall V_1, V_2 \in \mathcal{F},\ V_1 \cap V_2 \in \mathcal{F}$;
    + $\forall V \in \mathcal{F}$ and $V \subseteq W \subseteq X$, $W \in \mathcal{F}$.

    A filter is **degenerate** if $\varnothing \in \mathcal{F}$ (equivalently
    $\mathcal{F} = \mathscr{P}(X)$).

!!! example "Example (Ultrafilter)"
    <a id="ex-ultrafilter"></a>

    For $Y \subseteq X$ the **principal filter** of $Y$ is
    $\mathcal{F}_Y = \{V \subseteq X \mid Y \subseteq V\}$.

!!! definition "Definition (Ultrafilter)"
    <a id="def-7-1-2"></a>
    <a id="def-ultrafilter"></a>

    An **ultrafilter** on $X$ is a non-degenerate filter $\mathcal{F}$ such
    that no non-degenerate filter $\mathcal{G}$ strictly contains
    $\mathcal{F}$. Equivalently, $\mathcal{F}$ is
    non-degenerate and for every $V \subseteq X$, either
    $V \in \mathcal{F}$ or $X \setminus V \in \mathcal{F}$.

!!! definition "Definition (Filter basis)"
    <a id="def-7-1-3"></a>
    <a id="def-filter-basis"></a>

    A **filter basis** on $X$ is a subset $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathscr{P}(X)$ such that for every $A, B \in \mathcal{S}$ there exists
    $C \in \mathcal{S}$ with $C \subseteq A \cap B$. The filter
    $\mathcal{F}_\mathcal{S}$ generated by $\mathcal{S}$ is

    $$
    \mathcal{F}_\mathcal{S} = \{U \subseteq X \mid \exists A \in \mathcal{S},\ A \subseteq U\}.
    $$

!!! example "Example (Fréchet or cofinite filter)"
    <a id="ex-frechet-filter-or-cofinite-filter-on-an-infinite"></a>

    The **Fréchet filter** (or cofinite filter) on an infinite set $X$ is
    $\mathcal{F} = \{U \subseteq X \mid X \setminus U \text{ is finite}\}$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Intersections and generated filters)"
    <a id="prop-intersections-and-generated-filters"></a>

    Nonempty intersections of filters are filters. For
    $\mathcal S\subseteq\mathscr P(X)$, the **generated filter** is the
    intersection of all filters containing $\mathcal S$. Explicitly it
    consists of supersets of finite intersections of members of
    $\mathcal S$, with the empty intersection interpreted as $X$.
    A nonempty filter basis reduces this to supersets of its individual
    members. Finite intersections of a generating family form a basis
    generating the same filter. The principal filter of $Y$ is generated
    by $\{Y\}$.

??? proof "Proof"
    The filter axioms hold in each member of an intersection, and every
    filter contains $X$. A filter containing $\mathcal S$ must contain
    all its finite intersections and their supersets. Conversely these
    supersets form a filter: intersect the two finite lists witnessing
    membership, and use upward closure. A basis refines every finite
    intersection by induction, giving the simpler formula.

!!! proposition "Proposition (The Fréchet filter and tails)"
    <a id="prop-the-frechet-filter-and-tails"></a>

    Cofinite subsets form a filter since
    $X\setminus(U\cap V)=(X\setminus U)\cup(X\setminus V)$.
    It is nondegenerate exactly when $X$ is infinite. For infinite
    $I,J\subseteq\mathbb N$, the tails $I_{\ge j}$, $j\in J$, form a
    basis of the Fréchet filter of $I$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Tails are nested and have finite complements. Any finite complement
    is bounded, and the unbounded set $J$ supplies a tail avoiding it.
    A superset of a cofinite set has a smaller finite complement.

!!! definition "Definition (Pseudometric and balls)"
    <a id="def-pseudometric-and-balls"></a>

    A **pseudometric** is $d:X\times X\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}$ with
    $d(x,x)=0$, symmetry, and the triangle inequality. It is a
    **metric** if $d(x,y)=0$ implies $x=y$.
    The **open ball** is $B(x,\varepsilon)=\{y\mid d(x,y)<\varepsilon\}$.
    Balls of positive radius form a basis of the **neighbourhood filter**
    $\mathcal V_x=\{U\subseteq X\mid\exists\varepsilon>0,\ B(x,\varepsilon)\subseteq U\}$.
    Any nonempty set of positive radii with infimum zero gives the same filter.

??? proof "Proof"
    Taking the smaller radius refines two balls. Given any positive
    radius $\delta$, a radius from the specified set is below $\delta$,
    so its ball is contained in $B(x,\delta)$.

!!! remark "Remark (Ultrafilter maximality)"
    <a id="rem-ultrafilter-maximality"></a>

    The earlier ultrafilter definition means there is **no strictly
    larger nondegenerate filter**. Equality with itself is permitted.
    The written condition $\mathcal F\not\subseteq\mathcal G$ must not
    be read as forbidding $\mathcal G=\mathcal F$.

## Order Limit

!!! definition "Definition (Order limit of a net)"
    <a id="def-7-2-1"></a>
    <a id="def-order-limit-of-a-net"></a>

    Let $(G, \leq)$ be a partially ordered set. A net $(g_i)_{i \in I}$ in
    $G$ (i.e. a family indexed by a directed set) is **increasing** if
    $g_i \leq g_j$ whenever $i \leq j$. The **order limit** of such a net
    is the supremum $\bigvee_{i \in I} g_i$, when it exists.

!!! definition "Definition (Partially ordered group, archimedean)"
    <a id="def-7-2-2"></a>
    <a id="def-partially-ordered-group-archimedean"></a>

    Let $(G, +, \leq)$ be a **partially ordered group**: a group with a
    partial order such that $a \leq b$ implies $a + c \leq b + c$ for all $c$.
    The group is **archimedean** if for every $a, b \in G$ with $a > 0$
    there exists $n \in \mathbb{N}$ with $n a \geq b$.

Order convergence in the source is defined for arbitrary mappings along
filters by inferior and superior limits. The increasing-net definition
above is a special case, rather than the definition for a general sequence.
Throughout the following construction $G$ is an order-complete poset.
Examples are $\overline{\mathbb R}$, $[0,+\infty]$, and
$(\mathscr P(\Omega),\subseteq)$.

!!! definition "Definition (Superior and inferior limits)"
    <a id="def-filter-order-limits"></a>

    For $f:X\to G$ and $U\subseteq X$, set
    $f^s(U)=\sup f(U)$ and $f^i(U)=\inf f(U)$. Define

    $$
    \limsup_{\mathcal F}f=\inf_{U\in\mathcal F}f^s(U),\qquad
    \liminf_{\mathcal F}f=\sup_{U\in\mathcal F}f^i(U).
    $$

    If these coincide, their common value is the **order limit**
    $\lim_{\mathcal F}f$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Bounds and computation on a basis)"
    <a id="prop-bounds-and-computation-on-a-basis"></a>

    For a nondegenerate filter,
    $f^i(V)\le f^s(U)$ for all $U,V\in\mathcal F$, hence
    $\liminf_{\mathcal F}f\le\limsup_{\mathcal F}f$.
    If $\mathcal B$ generates $\mathcal F$, the two formulas can be
    computed over $B\in\mathcal B$. In particular for a sequence on
    infinite $I\subseteq\mathbb N$ and any infinite $J\subseteq\mathbb N$,

    $$
    \limsup a_n=\inf_{j\in J}\sup_{n\in I_{\ge j}}a_n,\qquad
    \liminf a_n=\sup_{j\in J}\inf_{n\in I_{\ge j}}a_n.
    $$

??? proof "Proof"
    $U\cap V\ne\varnothing$, so
    $f^s(U)\ge f^s(U\cap V)\ge f^i(U\cap V)\ge f^i(V)$.
    Take first an infimum over $U$ and then a supremum over $V$.
    For basis computation, every filter member contains a basis member,
    whose supremum is no larger and whose infimum is no smaller; the
    reverse bound follows from $\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal F$.

!!! example "Example (Oscillation and a decreasing sequence)"
    <a id="ex-oscillation-and-a-decreasing-sequence"></a>

    $( (-1)^n )$ has superior limit $1$ and inferior limit $-1$.
    For $(1/n)_{n\ge1}$, the supremum on the tail beginning at $j$ is
    $1/j$, while its infimum is $0$; both limiting quantities are $0$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Eventual inequalities)"
    <a id="prop-eventual-inequalities"></a>

    If $f\le g$ on some $A\in\mathcal F$, then
    $\limsup_{\mathcal F}f\le\limsup_{\mathcal F}g$ and
    $\liminf_{\mathcal F}f\le\liminf_{\mathcal F}g$.

??? proof "Proof"
    The members of $\mathcal F$ contained in $A$ form a basis: refine
    $U$ by $U\cap A$. On this basis both pointwise extrema obey the
    inequality, and taking their outer bounds preserves it.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Squeeze and monotone convergence)"
    <a id="thm-squeeze-and-monotone-convergence"></a>

    If $f\le g\le h$ eventually along a nondegenerate filter and
    $\lim f=\lim h=l$, then $\lim g=l$.
    An increasing sequence in $G$ converges to its supremum; a decreasing
    sequence converges to its infimum.

??? proof "Proof"
    Squeezing bounds both $\liminf g$ and $\limsup g$ between $l$.
    For increasing $(a_n)$, put $l=\sup_n a_n$. Then
    $\limsup a_n\le l$, while $\inf_{n\in I_{\ge j}}a_n=a_j$ for
    $j\in I$, giving $\liminf a_n=l$. Reverse the order for a
    decreasing sequence.

!!! example "Example (Factorials dominate a fixed exponential)"
    <a id="ex-factorials-dominate-a-fixed-exponential"></a>

    For $a>1$, choose an integer $N\ge2a$. For $n\ge N$,

    $$
    0\le\frac{a^n}{n!}
    =\frac{a^N}{N!}\prod_{k=N+1}^n\frac ak
    \le\frac{a^N}{N!}\,2^{-(n-N)}.
    $$

    Squeezing gives $a^n/n!\to0$.

!!! definition "Definition (Trace filter and subsequence)"
    <a id="def-trace-filter-and-subsequence"></a>

    The **trace** on $Y\subseteq X$ is
    $\mathcal F|_Y=\{U\cap Y\mid U\in\mathcal F\}$.
    A basis traces to a basis generating this filter. Tracing a Fréchet
    filter to an infinite subset gives its Fréchet filter; restricting a
    sequence to such a subset gives a **subsequence**.

??? proof "Proof"
    Intersections commute with trace. If $U\cap Y\subseteq W\subseteq Y$,
    then $U\cup W\in\mathcal F$ and $(U\cup W)\cap Y=W$, proving
    upward closure. A refining basis remains refining after intersection
    with $Y$. A cofinite trace is cofinite; any cofinite subset of $Y$
    is the trace of $X$ with its finite complement removed.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Limits under restriction)"
    <a id="prop-limits-under-restriction"></a>

    $\limsup_{\mathcal F|_Y}f|_Y\le\limsup_{\mathcal F}f$ and
    $\liminf_{\mathcal F|_Y}f|_Y\ge\liminf_{\mathcal F}f$.
    If the trace is nondegenerate, a limit is preserved by restriction.
    If $Y\in\mathcal F$, the two limiting extrema themselves are unchanged.

??? proof "Proof"
    On $U\cap Y$, suprema decrease and infima increase. A preserved
    nondegenerate trace places both between the original common limit.
    If $Y\in\mathcal F$, the traced sets form a basis for the original
    filter as well, so the basis formulas give equalities.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Monotone subsequences realizing limiting extrema)"
    <a id="thm-monotone-subsequences-realizing-limiting-extrema"></a>

    In a complete total order every sequence has a monotone subsequence
    converging to its superior limit and one converging to its inferior limit.

??? proof "Proof"
    Let $J=\{n\in I\mid a_n\ge a_m\text{ for every }m\ge n\}$.
    If $J$ is infinite, it gives a decreasing subsequence whose tail
    maxima are $a_n$, so its limit is the superior limit.
    If $J$ is finite, start beyond it and recursively choose $n_{k+1}$
    with $a_{n_{k+1}}$ greater than the maximum over
    $I\cap[n_0,n_k]$. Such an index exists because an index attaining
    that finite maximum is not in $J$, and it lies beyond $n_k$.
    The selected values increase to $\beta$. Every sufficiently late
    original term is bounded by a later selected term, so
    $\limsup a_n\le\beta$. The restriction inequality gives the reverse
    bound. Reverse the order for the inferior limit.

## Ordered Groups and Their Enhancement

!!! definition "Definition (Strictly ordered algebraic structures)"
    <a id="def-strictly-ordered-algebraic-structures"></a>

    The source calls a semigroup **partially ordered** when both left
    and right translations preserve strict inequalities. For a group
    this is equivalent to translation invariance of the nonstrict order.
    The opposite operation has the same property.

!!! example "Example (Ordered operations)"
    <a id="ex-ordered-operations"></a>

    $(\mathbb R,+,\le)$ and $(\mathbb R_{>0},\cdot,\le)$ are ordered
    groups. $(\mathbb N_{>0},\cdot,\mid)$ is an ordered **monoid**,
    rather than a group.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Translation, cancellation, and inversion)"
    <a id="prop-translation-cancellation-and-inversion"></a>

    If $a_1\le a_2$ and $b_1\le b_2$, then
    $a_1b_1\le a_2b_2$; a strict inequality in either factor makes it
    strict in the source's strictly ordered semigroup convention.
    Comparisons can be cancelled through a unit, or through any element
    in a total order. A totally ordered semigroup is cancellative.
    Inversion on units is strictly decreasing. A nontrivial totally
    ordered group has neither a greatest nor a least element.

??? proof "Proof"
    Use $a_1b_1\le a_2b_1\le a_2b_2$. Cancel a unit by multiplying
    by its inverse. In a total order, failure of $x\le y$ gives
    $x>y$ and thus the reverse strict translated inequality.
    Cancellation of equality follows by using both comparisons.
    From $x<y$ multiply successively by $x^{-1}$ and $y^{-1}$ to get
    $y^{-1}<x^{-1}$. A nontrivial group has an element $u>e$, by
    inverting a negative one if needed. If $b$ were greatest, then
    $bu>b$; reverse the order to exclude a least element.

!!! definition "Definition (Enhancement and partial operations)"
    <a id="def-enhancement-and-partial-operations"></a>

    For an ordered semigroup with neither extreme, put
    $\overline S=S\cup\{\bot,\top\}$ with $\bot<s<\top$.
    Extend the operation by $s\bot=\bot s=\bot$ for
    $s\in S\cup\{\bot\}$ and $s\top=\top s=\top$ for
    $s\in S\cup\{\top\}$. The products $\bot\top$ and $\top\bot$
    are **undefined**. For subsets $A,B$, $AB$ contains all defined
    products. For additive $\mathbb R$, the extremes are $-\infty,+\infty$;
    for multiplicative $\mathbb R_{>0}$, they are $0,+\infty$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Regrouping and order in the enhancement)"
    <a id="prop-regrouping-and-order-in-the-enhancement"></a>

    A finite product is defined exactly when its factors do not contain
    both extremes; every regrouping has the same domain and value.
    Definedness is symmetric in two factors, and commutativity extends
    where defined. Translation by an element of $S$ preserves strict
    comparisons in $\overline S$. If $x<a$ and $y<b$, both $xy$ and
    $ab$ are defined and $xy<ab$. If $x\le a$, $y\le b$ and both
    products are defined, then $xy\le ab$.

??? proof "Proof"
    A product containing only one extreme equals that extreme; without
    extremes use associativity in $S$. For a strict pairwise comparison,
    the lower factors cannot be $\top$ and the upper ones cannot be
    $\bot$. If an extreme occurs the claimed inequality follows directly;
    otherwise use the two-translation argument. For nonstrict comparisons
    separate the cases with extremes, and use monotonicity for finite factors.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Extended inversion and translated bounds)"
    <a id="prop-extended-inversion-and-translated-bounds"></a>

    Put $\bot^{-1}=\top$, $\top^{-1}=\bot$. For a unit or extreme $a$,
    $a<b$ is equivalent to definedness and $e<a^{-1}b$, and also to
    definedness and $e<ba^{-1}$. Inversion reverses strict comparisons.
    For a unit or extreme $b$, whenever the right side is defined,

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \sup(Ab)&=(\sup A)b,&\inf(Ab)&=(\inf A)b,\\
    \sup(bA)&=b(\sup A),&\inf(bA)&=b(\inf A).
    \end{aligned}
    $$

??? proof "Proof"
    For finite units use multiplication by the inverse; extreme cases
    follow from the table of defined products. A unit translates upper
    bounds bijectively: $ab\le M$ exactly when $a\le Mb^{-1}$, so
    translating a least upper bound gives a least upper bound. For an
    extreme use the fact that every defined product has that extreme
    as value, with the empty-set cases determined by definedness of the
    right side. Left versions use the opposite operation and lower-bound
    versions use the reversed order.

!!! example "Example (A nonunit translation need not preserve a supremum)"
    <a id="ex-a-nonunit-translation-need-not-preserve-a-supremum"></a>

    In the additive monoid $S=\{0\}\cup[2,3[\cup[4,+\infty[$,
    $A=[2,3[$ has $\sup_S A=4$, but $A+2=[4,5[$ has supremum $5$,
    whereas $\sup_S A+2=6$. The translator $2$ is not a unit of this monoid.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Suprema of products of sets)"
    <a id="thm-suprema-of-products-of-sets"></a>

    In an enhanced ordered group, whenever the right sides are defined,
    $\sup(AB)=(\sup A)(\sup B)$ and
    $\inf(AB)=(\inf A)(\inf B)$.
    For pointwise products on their domain of definedness,
    $\sup(fg)\le(\sup f)(\sup g)$ and
    $\inf(fg)\ge(\inf f)(\inf g)$ whenever these bounds exist.

??? proof "Proof"
    Pairwise monotonicity bounds every product by the product of the
    suprema. If either supremum is $\bot$, the defined product of
    suprema is $\bot$ and the conclusion follows; if one is $\top$,
    fix a nonbottom factor from the other set and use translated bounds
    to obtain supremum $\top$. Otherwise take the supremum over one
    factor by the translation lemma and then over the other, using the
    supremum-of-unions formula. Reverse the order for infima.
    The pointwise product image is a subset of the independent set product,
    giving the inequalities.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Limiting inequalities for the group operation)"
    <a id="thm-limiting-inequalities-for-the-group-operation"></a>

    In an enhanced ordered group with complete enhanced order, let
    $f,g:X\to\overline S$ have defined pointwise products and let
    $\mathcal F$ be nondegenerate. Each inequality holds when its
    right side is defined:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \limsup(fg)&\le(\limsup f)(\limsup g),\\
    \limsup(fg)&\ge(\limsup f)(\liminf g),\\
    \liminf(fg)&\ge(\liminf f)(\liminf g),\\
    \liminf(fg)&\le(\liminf f)(\limsup g).
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    All limits here are along $\mathcal F$.

??? proof "Proof"
    On a filter member $U$, the pointwise supremum is at most
    $f^s(U)g^s(U)$ when defined. The independent infima over $U,V$
    of $f^s(U)g^s(V)$ equal $(\limsup f)(\limsup g)$ by the set-product
    theorem. Refine both filter members by $U\cap V$ to compare with
    the diagonal expressions. Undefined intermediate pairs contain opposite
    extremes; any defined bounding pair in that case is $\top$, so it
    does not invalidate the upper bound.
    For the mixed lower bound fix $V\in\mathcal F$. On $U\cap V$,
    $f(x)g(x)\ge f(x)g^i(V)$ where defined. Take the supremum over
    $x$, then the infimum over $U$, and finally the supremum over $V$;
    the translation and product theorems give the second inequality.
    Extreme cases are checked from the operation table. Reverse the
    order to obtain the two inferior-limit inequalities.

!!! corollary "Corollary (One convergent factor)"
    <a id="cor-one-convergent-factor"></a>

    If $g$ has a limit, then
    $\limsup(fg)=(\limsup f)(\lim g)$ and
    $\liminf(fg)=(\liminf f)(\lim g)$ whenever the right side is defined.
    In particular two convergent factors have the expected product limit.

??? proof "Proof"
    The two corresponding bounds coincide because
    $\liminf g=\limsup g=\lim g$.

!!! example "Example (Perturbing an oscillation and exponential decay)"
    <a id="ex-perturbing-an-oscillation-and-exponential-decay"></a>

    $\limsup((-1)^n+1/n)=1$. For $a=1+b>1$, Bernoulli's inequality
    gives $a^n\ge nb$, hence $0\le\sqrt n/a^n\le1/(b\sqrt n)\to0$.
    Every fixed positive integer power $(\sqrt n/a^n)^k$ also tends to zero.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Maximum, minimum, and inversion)"
    <a id="prop-maximum-minimum-and-inversion"></a>

    In a complete total order put $f\vee g=\max(f,g)$ and
    $f\wedge g=\min(f,g)$. Then
    $\limsup(f\vee g)=\max(\limsup f,\limsup g)$ and
    $\liminf(f\wedge g)=\min(\liminf f,\liminf g)$.
    If both functions converge, their maximum and minimum converge to
    the maximum and minimum of their limits.
    In an enhanced ordered group,
    $\inf(A^{-1})=(\sup A)^{-1}$ and its dual; consequently
    $\limsup(f^{-1})=(\liminf f)^{-1}$ and
    $\liminf(f^{-1})=(\limsup f)^{-1}$.

??? proof "Proof"
    For the maximum split filter members according to whether
    $f^s(U)\ge g^s(U)$. If the first class refines every member, it is
    a basis and the limiting supremum of the maximum equals that of
    $f$. Otherwise restrict to a member which contains no first-class
    refinement; the second class becomes a basis and gives that of $g$.
    Monotonicity also bounds it below by both limiting suprema.
    Reverse the order for minimum. For convergent functions the matching
    bounds squeeze each operation to the corresponding operation on limits.
    Inversion exchanges upper and lower bounds bijectively, proving the
    set identities; apply them twice in the filter formulas.

## Absolute Values

!!! definition "Definition (Absolute value on a field)"
    <a id="def-7-3-1"></a>
    <a id="def-absolute-value-on-a-field"></a>

    Let $K$ be a field. An **absolute value** on $K$ is a map
    $|\cdot| : K \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ such that

    1. $|x| = 0 \iff x = 0$;
    2. $|xy| = |x| |y|$;
    3. $|x + y| \leq |x| + |y|$.

    The pair $(K, |\cdot|)$ is a **valued field**. The absolute value is
    **non-archimedean** if $|x + y| \leq \max(|x|, |y|)$ for all $x, y \in K$.

!!! example "Example (Standard, p-adic and trivial absolute values)"
    <a id="ex-the-standard-absolute-value-on"></a>

    + The standard absolute value on $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$.
    + The $p$-adic absolute value on $\mathbb{Q}$ for each prime $p$:
      $|x|_p = p^{-v_p(x)}$.
    + The trivial absolute value $|x| = 1$ for $x \neq 0$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Ostrowski's theorem)"
    <a id="thm-7-3-2"></a>
    <a id="thm-ostrowski-s-theorem"></a>

    Every non-trivial absolute value on $\mathbb{Q}$ is equivalent either to
    the standard archimedean one or to some $p$-adic absolute value.

??? proof "Proof"
    First suppose the absolute values of positive integers are bounded by $C$. Since $|m|^k=|m^k|\le C$, every integer has absolute value at most one. The binomial expansion and the bound on its integer coefficients give

    $$
    |x+y|^k\le(k+1)\max(|x|,|y|)^k.
    $$

    Taking $k$-th roots and letting $k\to\infty$ proves the ultrametric inequality. Nontriviality on $\mathbb Q$ implies that some positive integer has absolute value less than one, so one of its prime factors $p$ does too. There cannot be two such primes: Bezout would give $ap+bq=1$ and then $1\le\max(|p|,|q|)<1$. Every integer coprime to $p$ therefore has value one. Factoring numerator and denominator shows $|x|=|p|^{v_p(x)}=|x|_p^c$, where $c=-\log|p|/\log p>0$.

    Otherwise choose an integer $b\ge2$ with $|b|>1$. For each integer $a\ge2$, write $a^k$ in base $b$. If $M_b=\max_{0\le j<b}|j|$ and $r\le k\log a/\log b$ is the largest digit position, the triangle inequality gives

    $$
    |a|^k\le M_b\sum_{j=0}^r|b|^j\le C_b|b|^{k\log a/\log b}.
    $$

    Taking roots gives $|a|\le a^\alpha$, $\alpha=\log|b|/\log b>0$. If $|a|\le1$, the base-$a$ expansion of $b^k$ instead gives $|b|^k\le M_a(1+k\log b/\log a)$, a contradiction for large $k$. Hence $|a|>1$, and exchanging $a,b$ in the preceding bound gives the reverse inequality. Thus $|a|=a^\alpha$ for every positive integer, and multiplicativity extends this to $|x|=|x|_\infty^\alpha$ on $\mathbb Q$. Since $|2|\le2$, $0<\alpha\le1$. Positive powers induce the same topology, proving the classification.

The field absolute value introduced earlier differs from the
order-theoretic absolute value in this part of the source. Here $R$ is a
totally ordered abelian group, written additively, with complete enhancement
when limiting extrema are used.

!!! definition "Definition (Order absolute value and boundedness)"
    <a id="def-order-absolute-value-and-boundedness"></a>

    For $x\in\overline R$ put $|x|=\max(x,-x)$. Thus $|-x|=|x|$.
    A subset $A\subseteq R$ is **bounded** if $|a|\le M$ for all
    $a\in A$ and some $M>0$; a mapping is bounded if its image is.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Triangle and reverse triangle inequalities)"
    <a id="prop-triangle-and-reverse-triangle-inequalities"></a>

    Whenever the expressions are defined,
    $|a+b|\le|a|+|b|$,
    $\bigl||a|-|b|\bigr|\le|a-b|$, and
    $\bigl||a|-|b|\bigr|\le|a+b|$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Both $a+b$ and $-(a+b)$ are bounded by $|a|+|b|$.
    The triangle inequality applied to $(a-b)+b$ gives
    $|a|-|b|\le|a-b|$, and exchanging $a,b$ gives the negative bound.
    Replace $b$ by $-b$ for the last inequality. Defined extreme cases
    are immediate from the value $+\infty$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Absolute error and the epsilon criterion)"
    <a id="thm-absolute-error-and-the-epsilon-criterion"></a>

    For $f:X\to R$, $l\in R$, and nondegenerate $\mathcal F$,
    $\lim_{\mathcal F}f=l$ is equivalent to
    $\limsup_{\mathcal F}|f-l|=0$. These imply
    $\forall\varepsilon>0,\ \exists U\in\mathcal F,\ \forall x\in U,\ |f(x)-l|<\varepsilon$.
    The converse holds if $\inf R_{>0}=0$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Translation and inversion give the limiting extrema of $f-l$ and
    $l-f$. The maximum formula gives
    $\limsup|f-l|=\max(\limsup f-l,l-\liminf f)$.
    If this is zero, $l\le\liminf f\le\limsup f\le l$.
    An infimum of nonnegative suprema equal to zero supplies one
    supremum below every positive $\varepsilon$. Conversely the epsilon
    condition bounds the limiting supremum by every positive
    $\varepsilon$; take their infimum.

!!! corollary "Corollary (A finite limit gives eventual boundedness)"
    <a id="cor-a-finite-limit-gives-eventual-boundedness"></a>

    In a nontrivial $R$, a mapping with limit $l\in R$ is bounded on
    some filter member: choose $\varepsilon>0$ and use
    $|f|\le|f-l|+|l|<\varepsilon+|l|$ there. In the trivial group the
    mapping is identically zero and bounded in the usual nonnegative-bound
    convention.

## Ordered Rings and Real Places

!!! definition "Definition (Place of a field)"
    <a id="def-7-4-1"></a>
    <a id="def-place-of-a-field"></a>

    For the number-field discussion here, a **place** is an equivalence
    class of nontrivial absolute values inducing the same topology.
    An **archimedean** place comes from a real or complex embedding;
    a **real place** is specifically one arising from a real embedding.
    Finite places are **non-archimedean**. This absolute-value convention
    should be distinguished from a field-theoretic place defined as a
    partially defined homomorphism into another field.

!!! remark "Remark (Supplementary classification of number-field places)"
    <a id="thm-7-4-2"></a>
    <a id="rem-supplementary-classification-of-number-field-places"></a>

    Beyond the ordered-ring material in the lectures, the standard classification of absolute-value places says that real places of a number field $K$ correspond to its real embeddings.
    Complex places correspond to conjugate pairs of nonreal complex
    embeddings. These together are the archimedean, or infinite, places.
    Non-archimedean places correspond to nonzero prime ideals of
    $\mathcal{O}_K$. The complex infinite places are not non-archimedean.

!!! definition "Definition (Ordered ring and enhanced multiplication)"
    <a id="def-ordered-ring-and-enhanced-multiplication"></a>

    A nonzero unitary ring is **partially ordered** if its additive
    group is ordered and products of strictly positive elements are
    strictly positive. Positive multiplication on either side preserves
    inequalities, while negative multiplication reverses them.
    On $\overline R$, multiplying a nonzero signed element by infinity
    uses the usual sign rule; $0\cdot(\pm\infty)$ is undefined.
    Set $(+\infty)^{-1}=(-\infty)^{-1}=0$ for the extended reciprocal convention.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Signs and scalar embeddings)"
    <a id="prop-signs-and-scalar-embeddings"></a>

    In a totally ordered unitary ring, a nonzero square is positive,
    a unit and its inverse have the same sign, and $1>0$.
    The canonical homomorphism $\mathbb Z\to R$ is strictly increasing,
    so $R$ has characteristic zero. Any unitary homomorphism
    $\mathbb Q\to R$ is strictly increasing. Also $|ab|=|a||b|$.

??? proof "Proof"
    Either $a$ or $-a$ is positive, and both have square $a^2>0$.
    Since $a=a^{-1}a^2$, a unit's inverse has its sign. Applying the
    square assertion to $1$ gives positivity. If $m>n$, the difference
    $(m-n)1$ is a positive sum of copies of $1$.
    For positive denominators,
    $f(a/b)-f(c/d)=f(ad-bc)/(f(b)f(d))$ has the sign of $ad-bc$.
    Finally $|a||b|$ is the nonnegative one of $ab,-ab$.

!!! theorem "Theorem (Multiplication of convergent functions)"
    <a id="thm-multiplication-of-convergent-functions"></a>

    In a totally ordered unitary ring with complete enhancement, if
    $f,g:X\to R$ converge along a nondegenerate filter to $a,b\in\overline R$
    and $ab$ is defined, then $fg\to ab$.

??? proof "Proof"
    The source's error estimate treats finite $a,b$: eventual boundedness
    supplies $|f|\le M$, and

    $$
    |fg-ab|\le|f|\,|g-b|+|b|\,|f-a|
    \le M|g-b|+|b|\,|f-a|.
    $$

    This reduces the finite-limit assertion to errors tending to zero.
    In $\mathbb R$, for example, choose both errors smaller than
    $\varepsilon/(M+|b|+1)$ and apply the epsilon criterion.
    To cover the infinite limits omitted by that estimate, first fix the
    eventual signs. If one factor has a finite nonzero limit and the
    other tends to an infinity, its absolute value is bounded below by
    a positive constant, so the product tends to the infinity of the
    prescribed sign. If both factors tend to infinities, their absolute
    values eventually exceed every fixed positive bound; bounding one
    below by $1$ suffices. The undefined zero-times-infinity cases are
    excluded by the hypothesis.
