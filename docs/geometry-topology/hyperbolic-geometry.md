# Hyperbolic Geometry

This chapter establishes the basics of hyperbolic geometry via its two most
concrete models: the Riemann sphere / complex projective line on which the
(generalized) Möbius transformations act, and the upper half-plane model with
its hyperbolic metric. The main reference is [A+].

## The Riemann Sphere and the Complex Projective Plane

A line in $\mathbb{C}$ has an equation of the form

$$
(wz + \bar{w}\bar{z}) + c = 0
$$

for some $w \in \mathbb{C}$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$, and a circle in
$\mathbb{C}$ has an equation of the form

$$
z\bar{z} + (wz + \bar{w}\bar{z}) + c = 0
$$

for $w \in \mathbb{C}$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$. Consider the stereographic
projection

$$
\phi : S := S^2 - \{(0,0,1)\} \to \mathbb{C}, \quad (u, v, w) \mapsto \frac{u}{1-w} + i \frac{v}{1-w},
$$

which is a homeomorphism. Conversely, there is a way to "adjoin" a point to
$\mathbb{C}$ and recover the sphere $S^2$.

!!! definition "Definition (One-point compactification of $\mathbb{C}$)"
    <a id="def-6-1-3"></a>
    The **one-point compactification of $\mathbb{C}$** is the topological
    space whose underlying set is $\mathbb{C} \cup \{\infty\}$, with the
    topology generated by

    + the open sets of $\mathbb{C}$, and
    + the sets of the form $\{\infty\} \cup W$, where $W$ is the complement of
      a compact set in $\mathbb{C}$.

    It is denoted by $\bar{\mathbb{C}}$. The map $\phi$ extends to a
    homeomorphism $\bar{\phi} : S^2 \to \bar{\mathbb{C}}$.

!!! remark "Remark"
    <a id="rem-6-1-6"></a>
    More generally, for polynomials $f$ and $g$ and $z \in \mathbb{C}$ such
    that $f(z) \neq 0$ and $g(z) = 0$, the function $f/g$ extends to $z$ by
    $(f/g)(z) = \infty$.

In view of the line and circle equations, one may regard lines and circles as
unified geometric objects with equations of the form

$$
a z\bar{z} + (wz + \bar{w}\bar{z}) + c = 0
$$

for $w \in \mathbb{C}$ and $a, c \in \mathbb{R}$, imposing the extra rule that
lines contain the point $\infty$, whereas circles don't.

We present another point of view on this unification. Recall that the complex
projective space $\mathbb{CP}^1$ is defined by

$$
\mathbb{CP}^1 = \{(z_1, z_2) \in \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} : (z_1, z_2) \neq (0, 0)\} / \{(z_1, z_2) \simeq (kz_1, kz_2)\},
$$

an element represented by $(z_1, z_2)$ being denoted by $[z_1 : z_2]$.

!!! proposition "Proposition"
    <a id="prop-6-1-7"></a>
    The map

    $$
    \psi : \bar{\mathbb{C}} \to \mathbb{CP}^1, \quad z \mapsto
    \begin{cases} [z : 1], & z \in \mathbb{C}, \\ [1 : 0], & z = \infty, \end{cases}
    $$

    is a homeomorphism. Its inverse is given by

    $$
    \psi^{-1} : \mathbb{CP}^1 \to \bar{\mathbb{C}}, \quad [z_1 : z_2] \mapsto
    \begin{cases} z_1/z_2, & z_2 \neq 0, \\ \infty, & z_2 = 0. \end{cases}
    $$

!!! definition "Definition (Circle in $\mathbb{CP}^1$)"
    <a id="def-6-1-9"></a>
    A **circle in $\mathbb{CP}^1$** is a subspace determined by the equation

    $$
    a z_1 \bar{z}_1 + (w z_1 \bar{z}_2 + \bar{w} \bar{z}_1 z_2) + c z_2 \bar{z}_2 = 0
    $$

    for $w \in \mathbb{C}$ and $a, c \in \mathbb{R}$, with $a, c, w$ not all
    zero. Under $\psi$, the image of a circle or a line is a circle.

!!! proposition "Proposition"
    <a id="prop-6-1-11"></a>
    Let $z_i$, $1 \leq i \leq 3$, be distinct points of $\bar{\mathbb{C}}$.
    Then there is a unique circle $C$ in $\bar{\mathbb{C}}$ such that
    $z_i \in C$ for all $i$.

??? proof "Proof"
    If none of the 3 points is $\infty$, then planar geometry tells us that
    they uniquely determine a circle. Otherwise, say $z_1 = \infty$, then the
    circle is the closure of the line passing through $z_2$ and $z_3$.

## The Möbius Transformations

Let $z = (z_1, z_2) \in \mathbb{C}^2$ be regarded as a column vector, and let
$[z]$ denote the point in $\mathbb{CP}^1$ represented by $z$. Let

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{C}^{2 \times 2},
$$

and let $[Az]$ denote the point represented by
$[az_1 + bz_2 : cz_1 + dz_2]$.

!!! definition "Definition (Möbius transformations)"
    <a id="def-6-2-1"></a>
    A **Möbius transformation** on $\mathbb{CP}^1$ is a homeomorphism of the
    form

    $$
    [z] \mapsto [Az]
    $$

    where $A$ is an invertible matrix. A **generalized Möbius
    transformation** is either a Möbius transformation or of the form

    $$
    [z] \mapsto [A\bar{z}]
    $$

    where $A$ is an invertible matrix.

!!! proposition "Proposition"
    <a id="prop-6-2-2"></a>
    Let $m$ be a generalized Möbius transformation, and let $C$ be a circle in
    $\mathbb{CP}^1$. Then $m(C)$ is a circle.

!!! proposition "Proposition"
    <a id="prop-6-2-3"></a>
    The generalized Möbius transformations form a group under composition, and
    the Möbius transformations form a subgroup.

The group of generalized Möbius transformations is called the **generalized
Möbius group** and denoted by $M$; its subgroup of Möbius transformations is
called the **Möbius group** and denoted by $M^+$. The definitions give rise to
actions of $M$ and $M^+$ on $\mathbb{CP}^1$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Triple transitivity)"
    <a id="prop-6-2-5"></a>
    Let $[\alpha_1 : \alpha_2], [\beta_1 : \beta_2], [\gamma_1 : \gamma_2]$ and
    $[\alpha_1' : \alpha_2'], [\beta_1' : \beta_2'], [\gamma_1' : \gamma_2']$ be
    two sets of distinct points in $\mathbb{CP}^1$. Then there is a unique
    $m \in M^+$ satisfying

    $$
    m([\alpha_1 : \alpha_2]) = [\alpha_1' : \alpha_2'], \quad
    m([\beta_1 : \beta_2]) = [\beta_1' : \beta_2'], \quad
    m([\gamma_1 : \gamma_2]) = [\gamma_1' : \gamma_2'].
    $$

??? proof "Proof"
    It suffices to consider the special case

    $$
    [\alpha_1' : \alpha_2'] = [1, 0], \quad [\beta_1' : \beta_2'] = [1, 1], \quad [\gamma_1' : \gamma_2'] = [0, 1].
    $$

    Then we let $m[z] = [Az]$ for

    $$
    A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}.
    $$

    A simple calculation shows that there is a unique $m$ as required,
    determined by

    $$
    a = \gamma_2, \quad b = -\gamma_1, \quad c = \frac{\gamma_2\beta_1 - \gamma_1\beta_2}{\alpha_2\beta_1 - \alpha_1\beta_2} \alpha_2, \quad d = -\frac{\gamma_2\beta_1 - \gamma_1\beta_2}{\alpha_2\beta_1 - \alpha_1\beta_2} \alpha_1.
    $$

    The uniqueness of $m$ is left as an exercise.

!!! remark "Remark"
    <a id="rem-6-2-8"></a>
    Working with $\bar{\mathbb{C}}$, the Möbius transformations are maps of the
    form

    $$
    z \mapsto \begin{cases} \dfrac{az + b}{cz + d}, & z \in \mathbb{C}, \\ \dfrac{a}{c}, & z = \infty, \end{cases}
    $$

    and the generalized Möbius transformations are either the Möbius
    transformations or the maps of the form

    $$
    z \mapsto \begin{cases} \dfrac{a\bar{z} + b}{c\bar{z} + d}, & z \in \mathbb{C}, \\ \dfrac{a}{c}, & z = \infty, \end{cases}
    $$

    where in both cases the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$
    is invertible. We will use this presentation primarily for the rest of the
    chapter. The unique Möbius transformation $m$ satisfying
    $m(z_1) = 0$, $m(z_2) = 1$ and $m(z_3) = \infty$ is of the form

    $$
    m(z) = \frac{z - z_1}{z - z_3} \frac{z_2 - z_3}{z_2 - z_1}.
    $$

## The Cross Ratio

!!! definition "Definition (Cross ratio in $\bar{\mathbb{C}}$)"
    <a id="def-6-3-1"></a>
    Let $z_i$, $1 \leq i \leq 4$, be distinct points in $\bar{\mathbb{C}}$.
    The **cross ratio** of $z_1, \dots, z_4$ is

    $$
    (z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4) = \frac{(z_1 - z_4)(z_3 - z_2)}{(z_1 - z_2)(z_3 - z_4)}.
    $$

    If one of the $z_i$'s is $\infty$, take the corresponding limit.

!!! proposition "Proposition"
    <a id="prop-6-3-2"></a>
    For $m \in M^+$ and $z_i \in \bar{\mathbb{C}}$, $1 \leq i \leq 4$, we have

    $$
    (z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4) = (m(z_1), m(z_2), m(z_3), m(z_4)).
    $$

!!! corollary "Corollary"
    <a id="cor-6-3-3"></a>
    For $m \in M - M^+$ and $z_i$ as above, we have

    $$
    (z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4) = \overline{(m(z_1), m(z_2), m(z_3), m(z_4))}.
    $$

!!! proposition "Proposition"
    <a id="prop-6-3-4"></a>
    Let $z_i$ be as above. Then the $z_i$'s are cyclic quadrilaterals (i.e.
    lie on the same circle) if and only if

    $$
    (z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4) \in \mathbb{R}.
    $$

??? proof "Proof"
    By the proposition, it suffices to consider the case
    $z_2 = \infty$, $z_3 = 1$, $z_4 = 0$. Then

    $$
    \lim_{z_2 \to \infty} \frac{z_1(1 - z_2)}{z_1 - z_2} = z_1,
    $$

    and we conclude.

!!! corollary "Corollary"
    <a id="cor-6-3-5"></a>
    Let $m \in M$ and let $C$ be a circle in $\bar{\mathbb{C}}$. Then $m(C)$ is
    a circle.

By the proposition, if a circle $C$ contains three distinct points
$z_i$, $1 \leq i \leq 3$, then $C$ divides $\bar{\mathbb{C}}$ into two disjoint
open sets

$$
\{z \in \bar{\mathbb{C}} : (z, z_1, z_2, z_3) > 0\}
\quad \text{and} \quad
\{z \in \bar{\mathbb{C}} : (z, z_1, z_2, z_3) < 0\},
$$

each of which we call an (open) **disk**.

!!! corollary "Corollary"
    <a id="cor-6-3-7"></a>
    Let $D_i$, $i = 1, 2$, be a disk with boundaries $\partial D_i$. If
    $m \in M$ satisfies $m(D_1) = D_2$, then $m(\partial D_1) = \partial D_2$.

## The Conformality of Elements of $M^+$

Let $m$ be a Möbius transformation defined by

$$
m(z) = \frac{az + b}{cz + d}, \quad ad - bc \neq 0.
$$

Regarding $m$ as a smooth function from $\mathbb{R}^2 - \{-d/c\}$ to
$\mathbb{R}^2$, the derivative of $m$ is the $2 \times 2$ real matrix

$$
m' = \begin{pmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y \end{pmatrix}
$$

where $m(x + iy) = u + iv$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Cauchy–Riemann formula)"
    <a id="prop-6-4-1"></a>
    The matrix $m'$ satisfies

    $$
    u_x = v_y, \quad u_y = -v_x,
    $$

    or equivalently

    $$
    m' = r \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix},
    $$

    i.e. $m'$ is a rotation matrix.

!!! definition "Definition (Conformal map)"
    <a id="def-6-4-2"></a>
    A **conformal map** from an open set $U$ of $\bar{\mathbb{C}}$ to
    $\bar{\mathbb{C}}$ is a smooth map $f$ such that for two smooth paths
    $\phi$ and $\psi$ in $U$ intersecting at $p \in U$, the angle between the
    tangent vectors of $\phi$ and $\psi$ at $p$ equals the angle between the
    tangent vectors of $f \cdot \phi$ and $f \cdot \psi$ at $f(p)$. If
    $p = \infty$, replace $\phi$ and $\psi$ with $f \cdot \phi$ and
    $f \cdot \psi$, respectively, and replace $p$ with $0$; similarly if
    $f(p) = \infty$, replace $f$ by $1/f$.

!!! proposition "Proposition"
    <a id="prop-6-4-3"></a>
    The Möbius transformation is a conformal map.

??? proof "Proof"
    When $\infty$ is not involved, this is a consequence of the
    Cauchy–Riemann formula. To deal with $\infty$, see Exercise VI.4.4.

If $m \in M - M^+$, then $m$ inverts the angles between tangent lines.

## The Upper Half-Plane Model of Hyperbolic Geometry

Let

$$
\mathbb{H} := \{z \in \mathbb{C} : \operatorname{Im} z > 0\}
$$

be the upper half-plane.

!!! definition "Definition (Hyperbolic line)"
    <a id="def-6-5-1"></a>
    A **(hyperbolic) line in $\mathbb{H}$** is either a half line
    perpendicular to $\mathbb{R}$, or a half circle of which the center lies
    in $\mathbb{R}$. Two lines in $\mathbb{H}$ are **parallel** if they are
    disjoint.

!!! proposition "Proposition"
    <a id="prop-6-5-2"></a>
    For any distinct points $\alpha, \beta$ in $\mathbb{H}$, there is a unique
    line $L$ passing through $\alpha, \beta$.

!!! proposition "Proposition (Playfair's axiom fails)"
    <a id="prop-6-5-3"></a>
    Let $L$ be a line in $\mathbb{H}$, and let $p \in \mathbb{H}$. Then there
    are infinitely many lines containing $p$ parallel to $L$.

We consider the (generalized) Möbius transformations that map $\mathbb{H}$ to
itself. Let

$$
M_{\mathbb{H}} := \{m \in M : m(\mathbb{H}) = \mathbb{H}\}, \qquad
M_{\mathbb{H}}^+ := \{m \in M^+ : m(\mathbb{H}) = \mathbb{H}\}.
$$

!!! proposition "Proposition"
    <a id="prop-6-5-5"></a>
    The elements of $M_{\mathbb{H}}^+$ are the elements $m$ of the form

    $$
    m(z) = \frac{az + b}{cz + d}
    $$

    for $a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $ad - bc > 0$. The elements of
    $M_{\mathbb{H}}$ are either in $M_{\mathbb{H}}^+$ or of the form

    $$
    m(z) = \frac{a\bar{z} + b}{c\bar{z} + d}
    $$

    for $a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $ad - bc < 0$.

??? proof "Proof"
    By Corollary 6.3.7 we have $M_{\mathbb{H}} \subset M_{\mathbb{R}}$ and
    $M_{\mathbb{H}}^+ \subset M_{\mathbb{R}}^+$; the rest is a routine
    computation.

!!! proposition "Proposition"
    <a id="prop-6-5-6"></a>
    The elements of $M_{\mathbb{H}}$ take hyperbolic lines to hyperbolic
    lines.

!!! proposition "Proposition"
    <a id="prop-6-5-7"></a>
    The group $M_{\mathbb{H}}$ acts transitively on $\mathbb{H}$.

## Length and Distance in $\mathbb{H}$

Throughout this section, $I$ denotes a closed interval $[\alpha, \beta]$.

!!! definition "Definition (Hyperbolic length)"
    <a id="def-6-6-1"></a>
    Given a smooth curve $\sigma : I \to \mathbb{H}$, the **hyperbolic length**,
    or simply **length** if there is no risk of ambiguity, is the integral

    $$
    \operatorname{length}(\sigma) = \int_\alpha^\beta (\operatorname{Im} \sigma(t))^{-1} |\sigma'(t)| \, dt.
    $$

    A more convenient convention is

    $$
    \operatorname{length}(\sigma) = \int (\operatorname{Im} z)^{-1} |dz|,
    $$

    where we denote $\sigma(t)$ by $z$.

!!! proposition "Proposition"
    <a id="prop-6-6-2"></a>
    The length of a smooth curve is invariant under generalized Möbius
    transformations. More precisely, for $m \in M_{\mathbb{H}}^+$ and a smooth
    curve $\sigma$, we have

    $$
    \operatorname{length}(\sigma) = \operatorname{length}(m(\sigma)).
    $$

??? proof "Proof"
    Consider the case $m \in M_{\mathbb{H}}^+$; the general case is left as an
    exercise. Suppose $m(z) = \frac{az+b}{cz+d}$. We have

    $$
    |dm(z)| = \left| d\left( \frac{az + b}{cz + d} \right) \right| = \left| \frac{ad - bc}{(cz + d)^2} dz \right|,
    $$

    and

    $$
    \operatorname{Im}(mz) = \operatorname{Im}\left( \frac{az + b}{cz + d} \right) = |cz + d|^{-2} (ad - bc) \operatorname{Im} z.
    $$

    The desired result follows from the two formulas.

!!! remark "Remark"
    <a id="rem-6-6-3"></a>
    One may compare the role of the Euclidean group $SE(2)$, generated by
    rotations, reflections and parallel translations, with the generalized
    Möbius group $M_{\mathbb{H}}$. They are indeed both isometry groups, but
    with respect to different geometries: Euclidean geometry and hyperbolic
    geometry. In Euclidean geometry, the length of the line segment joining two
    given points $p$ and $q$ is the least among the lengths of all smooth
    curves joining $p$ and $q$, and defines the metric in the Euclidean plane.
    Similar facts hold in hyperbolic geometry.

!!! theorem "Theorem"
    <a id="thm-6-6-4"></a>
    Let $p, q \in \mathbb{H}$, and let $\sigma$ be the unique line segment
    joining $p, q$. Let $\delta(p, q)$ be the infimum of the length of all
    curves joining $p$ and $q$. Then we have

    $$
    \delta(p, q) = \operatorname{length}(\sigma).
    $$

    Furthermore, $\delta$ defines a metric over $\mathbb{H}$, making
    $\mathbb{H}$ a metric space.

!!! remark "Remark (The pseudosphere)"
    <a id="rem-6-6-5"></a>
    In Chapter V we considered the pseudosphere, a regular surface
    parametrized by

    $$
    \phi(u_1, u_2) = (u_2^{-1}\cos u_1, u_2^{-1}\sin u_1, \ln(u_2 + (u_2^2 - 1)^{1/2}) - u_2^{-1}(u_2^2 - 1)^{1/2})
    $$

    with $u_2 > 1$. Its first fundamental form is

    $$
    \frac{du_1^2 + du_2^2}{u_2^2},
    $$

    which is compatible with the line element $(\operatorname{Im} z)^{-1}|dz|$
    that gives the hyperbolic lengths, for $u_2 > 1$. Therefore the "intrinsic"
    geometry of $\mathbb{H}$, at least of its subspace $\{\operatorname{Im} z >
    1\}$, is the "same geometry" as that of the pseudosphere. By Exercise
    V.5.16 and the Theorema Egregium, we say that hyperbolic geometry is of
    **negative curvature**, although we do not "see" how $\mathbb{H}$ is bent.
